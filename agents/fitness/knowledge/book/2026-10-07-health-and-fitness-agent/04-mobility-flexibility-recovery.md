# Mobility, Flexibility, Warm-up and Recovery

> supplementation and any dietary recovery aid belong to the sibling nutritionist agent (see
> `07-nutrition-interface-boundary-note.md`).

## 1. The flexibility prescription

- ACSM's 2011 position stand: a series of flexibility exercises for each major muscle-tendon group
  totalling **60 seconds per exercise**, on **≥2 days/week**.[^4]
- Techniques named as acceptable include **static, proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) and
  ballistic** stretching.[^4]
- **Neuromotor** exercise (balance, agility, coordination, gait) is prescribed separately, on **2–3
  days/week**.[^4]
- WHO's older-adult recommendation folds balance into a multicomponent prescription on **≥3
  days/week**.[^2]

### Long-term range-of-motion gains

- A meta-analysis of **23 studies** found all stretching typologies improved range of motion (ROM)
  over the long term, with **static protocols producing significantly greater gains than ballistic or
  PNF**.[^36]
- The same analysis suggested a **minimum of 5 minutes per week per muscle group** of static
  stretching, and that stretching on **at least 5 days/week** may be beneficial for ROM
  improvement.[^36]

### Acute ROM gains: technique barely matters

- A systematic review with meta-analysis (Behm et al., *Sports Med Open* 2023;9:107) found **no
  significant difference between stretching techniques** for acute ROM (p = 0.72).[^34]
- Effect sizes were similar across modes: **static −0.570, ballistic/dynamic −0.447, PNF
  −0.581**.[^34]
- Overall, stretching beat control with a **small effect (ES −0.555, p < 0.001)**.[^34]
- Meta-regression found **no significant relationship between stretch duration and ROM gain**
  (R² = 0.00, p = 0.39); durations from **5 to 900 seconds** all produced significant
  improvements.[^34]
- The authors' conclusion: all types of stretching can be used acutely across diverse populations
  **with similar results expected**, regardless of intensity, duration, technique, sex, age or
  training status.[^34]

**Agent design implication.** For flexibility the agent should prescribe *total weekly time* and
*consistency*, not a specific hold length or technique — the evidence does not discriminate between
techniques acutely, and the long-term signal favours static done regularly.[^34][^36]

## 2. Static stretching before exercise — the dose, not the act, is the problem

- Short-duration static stretching (**≤60 s per muscle group**), especially inside a full warm-up with
  an aerobic component and dynamic stretching, **trivially impairs** subsequent strength and power:
  about **1–2%**.[^35]
- Longer durations (**>60 s per muscle group**) produce substantial, practically relevant declines of
  **4.0–7.5%**.[^35]
- One meta-analysis of 104 studies found static stretching reduced **maximal strength by 5.4%** and
  **power by 1.9%**.[^35]
- A systematic review of 125 studies reported **≥60 s per muscle group → 4.6% decline**, versus
  **≤60 s → 1.1%**.[^35]
- The authors recommend integrating short static stretching **within** a warm-up that also includes
  **aerobic activity, dynamic stretching and sport-specific activity**.[^35]
- The detrimental effects are "mainly limited to longer durations," which are **not typically used**
  in real pre-exercise routines.[^35]
- Separately: stretching showed **no significant injury-prevention benefit** in Lauersen's
  meta-analysis of injury-prevention RCTs.[^26]

**Agent design implication.** The honest line is: "a quick static stretch before training is not
harmful; holding stretches for more than a minute per muscle right before a maximal effort measurably
reduces performance; and stretching is not an injury-prevention strategy."[^26][^35]

## 3. Warm-up: the RAMP structure

- Jeffreys' **RAMP** framework has four phases: **Raise, Activate, Mobilise, Potentiate**.[^47]
- **Raise** elevates body temperature, heart rate, respiration rate, blood flow and joint-fluid
  viscosity via low-intensity activity.[^47]
- **Activate and Mobilise** is the second phase, and the evidence here favours **dynamic movement
  over static holds**.[^47]
- **Potentiate** primes the nervous system with progressively higher-intensity, task-specific
  work.[^47]
- In youth soccer players, a RAMP warm-up produced significant acute benefits for **sprint and
  vertical-jump performance**, and was more effective than static stretching or no warm-up for
  explosive power and neuromuscular readiness.[^47]
- This maps cleanly onto Chaabene et al.'s recommendation that a warm-up contain an aerobic component,
  dynamic stretching and sport-specific activity.[^35][^47]

## 4. DOMS: what it is, and what it is not

Reference: Di Lorenzo L, Forte AM, Agosti V, Forte F, Lanciano T, Pirraglia N, D'Avanzo C. *Advances
in non-pharmacological strategies for DOMS: a scoping and critical review of recent evidence.* J
Funct Morphol Kinesiol. 2025;10(4):452, doi 10.3390/jfmk10040452.[^37]

- DOMS is **localised muscle tenderness, stiffness and pain arising 24–72 h after eccentric or
  high-intensity exercise**.[^37]
- It is a self-limiting inflammatory response that peaks within **1–7 days** and typically resolves
  within a week.[^37]
- **DOMS does not result from lactic acid accumulation — that theory is now considered
  obsolete.**[^37]
- Current mechanisms: **microtrauma and sarcomere disruption** from eccentric loading, **perimysial
  connective-tissue damage**, **secondary inflammatory cascades** with cytokine release and oxidative
  stress, **nociceptor sensitisation** particularly in fascia, and **satellite-cell** involvement in
  regeneration.[^37]
- A network meta-analysis places **peak soreness at 24–72 h** with assessment time points at 0, 24,
  48 and 72 h post-exercise.[^38]

**Agent design implication.** "Lactic acid build-up" is the single most common myth the agent will
meet in this area, and it should be corrected directly with the mechanism above.[^37] A second myth
worth naming: soreness is not a measure of workout quality, since DOMS is driven by *unaccustomed*
eccentric loading rather than by effective training.[^37]

## 5. Recovery modalities: what the evidence actually ranks

Network meta-analysis: Hou C, Yin W, Qiao F. *Acute and delayed effects of post-exercise recovery
strategies on explosive performance and markers of muscle damage.* Healthcare. 2026;14(10):1321, doi
10.3390/healthcare14101321 — **22 RCTs**, time points 0/24/48/72 h.[^38]

| Outcome | Best-ranked strategy | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Countermovement jump, acute (24 h) | **active recovery**, then cold-water immersion[^38] | explosive-performance restoration |
| DOMS at 0 h | **massage** strongest, CWI also beneficial[^38] | immediate soreness relief |
| DOMS at 24 h | **massage** ranked first[^38] | advantage maintained |
| DOMS at 48 h | **only CWI** retained significant benefit[^38] | effects fade |
| Creatine kinase, acute | **CWI** was the only intervention that reduced it[^38] | muscle-damage marker |

- The authors' overall conclusion is that recovery efficacy is **strongly time-dependent**: active
  recovery for short-term explosive performance, massage for early soreness, CWI for broader
  short-term support.[^38]
- From the DOMS scoping review: **active recovery** and exercise-induced analgesia repeatedly
  attenuate DOMS discomfort, supporting structured low-intensity activity **over complete
  rest**.[^37]
- **Foam rolling**: benefits appear primarily mechanical (tissue tone, flexibility, elasticity) rather
  than analgesic; **pain reduction is inconsistent**.[^37]
- **Compression garments** are endorsed for both treatment and prevention, with early application
  potentially mitigating symptom onset.[^37]
- **Static stretching for DOMS**: an umbrella review concluded stretching protocols **do not offer
  meaningful clinical benefit** for DOMS management, and the review recommends de-emphasising
  it.[^37]
- **Contrast baths** do not appear to add therapeutic advantage over CWI alone.[^37]
- **Cryosauna** at **−110 °C applied 48–72 h post-exercise** significantly reduced muscle stiffness,
  leukocyte count and creatine kinase.[^37]

## 6. Cold-water immersion blunts adaptation — the important caveat

Reference: Piñero A, Burke R, Augustin F, et al. *Throwing cold water on muscle growth: a systematic
review with meta-analysis of the effects of postexercise cold water immersion on resistance
training-induced hypertrophy.* Eur J Sport Sci. 2024;24(2):177–189, doi 10.1002/ejsc.12074.[^39]

- **8 randomised controlled trials**. Resistance training alone produced greater hypertrophy than
  CWI + resistance training (**cSMD₀.₅ = −0.22, 95% CrI −0.47 to 0.04**), with a **95.7% probability**
  the difference favoured training alone, at small effect magnitude.[^39]
- Conclusion: CWI immediately after resistance training **may modestly attenuate hypertrophy
  gains**, consistently across trained and untrained participants.[^39]
- Practical advice from the authors: if muscle growth is the priority, **avoid CWI immediately after
  resistance training**; the finding applies **specifically to immediate post-exercise cooling**.[^39]
- Study quality was rated **generally fair to poor**, so the conclusion is directional rather than
  settled.[^39]
- Mechanistically, CWI reduces **mTORC1** signalling, shifting post-exercise protein balance toward
  reduced synthesis and increased breakdown, which blunts muscle-fibre hypertrophy.[^40]
- Effects differ by outcome: in some trials blunted fibre hypertrophy **did not** translate into
  impaired maximal strength development, while over longer periods CWI has been reported to reduce
  hypertrophy gains and blunt maximal isometric strength gains across a **12-week, twice-weekly**
  protocol.[^40]
- Narrative and meta-analytic reviews also report CWI may blunt gains in **absolute strength and
  muscular power**.[^40]

**Agent design implication.** The agent should separate the two uses of cold: as **acute symptom
relief and short-term performance recovery** (supported — best-ranked for 48 h soreness and for
CK),[^38] versus as a **routine post-lifting habit when the goal is muscle growth** (counterproductive
to a small degree).[^39][^40] The resolution is timing: not immediately after resistance sessions, and
not as a default.[^39]

## 7. Sleep

- Most elite athletes **do not get the recommended 7–9 hours** per night and average **less sleep than
  the general population**.[^41]
- The National Sleep Foundation recommendation for healthy adults 18–64 is **7–9 hours**.[^41]
- **Sleep extension of 46–113 minutes** in athletes habitually sleeping ~7 h is the range proposed as
  a general recommendation for extension programmes.[^42]
- Sleep extension improved measured performance including **sprint times, free-throw and three-point
  shooting accuracy, tennis serve accuracy and endurance capacity**.[^41][^42]
- **Napping 20–90 minutes** can improve performance after a normal night and restore performance
  decrements to baseline after partial sleep restriction.[^42]
- Sleep's effects on performance have been reviewed across physiological and molecular mechanisms,
  reinforcing it as a primary rather than peripheral recovery variable.[^43]

**Agent design implication.** Sleep is the highest-leverage, lowest-risk recovery recommendation the
agent can make, and the numbers above make it concrete ("aim to add 45–60 minutes" is more actionable
than "sleep more").[^41][^42] Persistent non-restorative sleep, loud snoring with witnessed apnoeas,
or daytime sleepiness that does not respond to extension is a **medical referral**, not a coaching
problem.[^12]

## 8. Rest days, deloads and detraining

- **Deload frequency in practice**: deloads are commonly pre-planned every **4–8 weeks**.[^77]
- **Typical deload week prescription** reported by strength and conditioning coaches: **1–2
  sessions**, **30–60 minutes**, **1–3 sets**, **1–6 repetitions**, at **60–84% 1RM**.[^45]
- **Evidence on mid-programme deloads is not favourable**: a 1-week deload at the midpoint of a
  9-week resistance programme **negatively influenced lower-body strength** while having **no effect
  on hypertrophy, power or local muscular endurance**.[^44]
- The traditional (no-deload) group showed greater improvements in both **isometric and dynamic
  lower-body strength** than the deload group.[^44]
- **Tapering near competition is different and does help**: an effective taper has produced **2–8%
  improvements** in strength performance.[^77]
- **Detraining**: a strength decrease during cessation of **under 7 days is unlikely**; maximal force
  declined at a similar rate for <7 days and 7–14 days, and the decrease became **significant only
  after the third week** of cessation.[^46]
- Time-course data on resistance training and detraining in older adults is available for the same
  question in that population.[^46]

**Agent design implication.** The agent should distinguish a **taper** (deliberate pre-event volume
reduction, supported) from a **deload** (routine mid-cycle week off, not supported for strength) and
from a **break** (missing up to a week costs essentially nothing).[^44][^46][^77] Rest days remain
necessary for the simpler reason that the guideline frequency is 2–3 resistance days per week, not
daily.[^4][^7]

## 9. Overtraining — what to watch, and when to refer

- **Functional overreaching**: short-term performance dip that **resolves into improvement** after
  recovery. Normal.[^12]
- **Non-functional overreaching**: training and recovery out of balance; the dip persists.[^12]
- **Overtraining syndrome**: separated from NFOR by clinical outcome and **exclusion diagnosis**
  only.[^12]
- **No marker is diagnostic** — hormonal, performance, psychological, biochemical and immune markers
  have all been assessed and none meets accepted criteria.[^12]
- Therefore: the agent describes the pattern and **refers**; it does not label a user as
  overtrained.[^12]

Practical coaching responses that stay inside scope: reduce volume rather than frequency, protect
sleep,[^41][^42] keep activity light rather than stopping entirely,[^37] and escalate if performance
and mood do not recover.[^12]
