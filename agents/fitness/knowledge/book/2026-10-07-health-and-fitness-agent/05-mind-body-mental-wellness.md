# Mind-Body Practices and Mental Wellness

> Scope note: this file is about practices a fitness agent may *teach or encourage* — meditation,
> mindfulness, breathwork, yoga, tai chi, and exercise used for mood and stress. It is **not** about
> treating mental illness. The escalation line is in section 7 and is not optional.

---

## 1. The evidence-strength landscape at a glance

| Practice | Strongest-evidence claims | Weakest / contested |
|---|---|---|
| Exercise for depression | large network meta-analysis, effects comparable to CBT[^53] | certainty rated low to very low[^53] |
| Mindfulness / meditation | works as well as evidence-based therapies for anxiety and depression[^48] | no long-term effects beyond ~2 months in one analysis[^48] |
| Yoga | substantial beneficial effect for anxiety; slightly better than no exercise for low-back pain[^49] | PTSD only "low-quality evidence of a possible benefit"[^49] |
| Tai chi | high-certainty 20% reduction in people who fall; strongly recommended for knee/hip OA[^50] | depression effect only "small"[^50] |
| Breathwork | significant pooled effects on stress, anxiety, depression[^52] | heterogeneous interventions, self-report outcomes[^52] |
| Qigong | benefits in fibromyalgia pain, sleep and function with diligent practice[^59] | small literature, specific populations[^59] |

## 2. Exercise as a mental-health intervention — the strongest single finding

Reference: Noetel M, Sanders T, Gallardo-Gómez D, et al. *Effect of exercise for depression:
systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.* BMJ. 2024 Feb
14;384:e075847, doi 10.1136/bmj-2023-075847.[^53]

- **218 unique studies, 495 arms, 14,170 participants.**[^53]
- Effect sizes versus active controls (Hedges' g, more negative = greater symptom reduction):
  - **Walking or jogging: −0.63** (95% CI −0.80 to −0.46)[^53]
  - **Yoga: −0.55** (−0.73 to −0.36)[^53]
  - **Strength training: −0.49** (−0.69 to −0.29)[^53]
  - **Mixed aerobic: −0.43** (−0.61 to −0.25)[^53]
  - **Tai chi / qigong: −0.42** (−0.65 to −0.21)[^53]
  - **Aerobic + psychotherapy: −0.54** (−0.76 to −0.32)[^53]
  - **Exercise + SSRIs: −0.55** (−0.86 to −0.23)[^53]
- Comparators in the same network: **CBT alone −0.55**, **SSRIs alone −0.26**.[^53]
- Effects were **proportional to prescribed intensity** and stronger for **group exercise** and for
  interventions with **clear prescriptions**.[^53]
- **Certainty**: walking/jogging rated **low confidence**; all other modalities **very low
  confidence**, driven by within-study bias.[^53]
- The authors recommend exercise be considered **alongside** psychotherapy and antidepressants as a
  core treatment.[^53]

**Agent design implication.** This licenses the agent to say, with numbers, that exercise is an
effective treatment for depressive symptoms — and obliges it to also report the low certainty and to
never position exercise as a **replacement** for care the person is already receiving.[^53]

### Stress and the acute mechanism

- Exercise initially spikes the stress response, but people show **lower levels of stress hormones
  such as cortisol and epinephrine after** bouts of physical activity.[^55]
- The neuromodulator **norepinephrine** is implicated: exercise increases its concentration in brain
  regions involved in the stress response, which may help the brain handle stress more
  efficiently.[^55]
- A regular exercise routine can decrease the effects of stress on the body, improve mental health and
  mood, and enhance memory and cognition.[^55]
- **About five minutes of aerobic exercise can begin to stimulate anti-anxiety effects.**[^55]

### What people actually report (useful for coaching, not for claims)

APA's *Stress in America* survey data:[^54]

- **53%** feel good about themselves after exercising; **35%** say it puts them in a good mood;
  **30%** report feeling less stressed.[^54]
- **43%** exercise specifically to manage stress, and of those, **62%** call it very or extremely
  effective.[^54]
- But **39%** of those who exercise to manage stress **skipped exercise in the past month when they
  were feeling stressed**.[^54]
- Only **17%** of adults exercise daily; **37%** exercise less than once a week or not at all.[^54]
- For comparison, **29%** of those using online activities and **33%** using TV/film rate those
  methods very or extremely effective.[^54]

**Agent design implication.** That 39% figure is the behavioural problem worth designing around: the
agent should expect adherence to drop exactly when stress is highest, and should pre-plan a reduced
minimum ("the five-minute version") rather than treating a missed week as failure.[^54][^55]

## 3. Meditation and mindfulness — NCCIH's read

Reference: NCCIH, *Meditation and Mindfulness: Effectiveness and Safety* (page last updated June
2022).[^48]

- **Anxiety and depression**: mindfulness-based approaches were **better than no treatment** and
  **worked as well as evidence-based therapies**.[^48]
- But a 2021 analysis found **mixed results for short-term effectiveness and no long-term effects**
  in studies lasting beyond two months.[^48]
- **Chronic pain**: mindfulness meditation was more effective than several other treatments, but the
  **studies were of low quality**.[^48]
- **Acute pain**: analyses found **no evidence of reduced pain severity**.[^48]
- **Blood pressure**: a 2020 review found MBSR associated with a **significant reduction** in blood
  pressure in people with health conditions; the American Heart Association considers meditation a
  **possible benefit** with effects undetermined.[^48]
- **Sleep**: mindfulness meditation improved sleep quality **more than education-based treatments**,
  and **matched** evidence-based approaches such as CBT.[^48]
- **PTSD**: six of eight studies reported symptom reduction; one military trial found meditation **as
  effective as prolonged exposure therapy**.[^48]
- **Smoking / substance use**: craving levels significantly decreased, but mindfulness was **not more
  effective at preventing relapse**.[^48]
- NCCIH's own hedging vocabulary — "may help", "mixed results", "evidence is not conclusive",
  "interpret with caution" — is a good model for the agent's register.[^48]
- NCCIH's standing caution: **do not use meditation or mindfulness to replace conventional care or as
  a reason to postpone seeing a provider about a medical problem.**[^48]

### Adverse events — the part most fitness content omits

- NCCIH: meditation and mindfulness "usually are considered to have few risks," but **few studies have
  examined harms**, so definite safety statements are not possible.[^48]
- A 2020 review of **83 studies** found **about 8% of participants had a negative effect** from
  practising meditation — **similar to the rate reported for psychological therapies**.[^48]
- The most commonly reported negative effects were **anxiety and depression**.[^48]
- For MBSR specifically, mindfulness practices were **not more harmful than receiving no
  treatment**.[^48]
- Farias et al.'s systematic review (*Acta Psychiatr Scand* 2020;142(5):374–393) screened **6,742
  studies** and found **under 1% defined or measured meditation adverse events**.[^51]
- Among the 83 studies that did assess them, the commonest adverse events were **anxiety (33%),
  depression (27%), cognitive anomalies (25%), and gastrointestinal problems or suicidal ideation
  (11% each)**.[^51]
- The review concluded adverse events are **common during or after meditation** and **may occur in
  people with no prior mental-health difficulties**.[^51]

**Agent design implication.** The agent must carry an adverse-event disclosure for meditation
content — specifically that intensive or prolonged practice can surface anxiety, depression,
dissociation or distressing memories, and that this is a documented phenomenon rather than a personal
failure.[^48][^51] Suicidal ideation appearing in the adverse-event list is why the escalation rule in
section 7 applies to meditation content and not only to exercise content.[^51]

### MBSR, for reference

- The standard **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction** programme is **8 weeks**, with weekly group
  classes of **2.5 hours (up to 3.5 h in some formats)**, plus an **all-day silent retreat** of about
  **7.5 hours**, conventionally in week 6.[^56]
- Home practice is **up to 45 minutes daily** using guided audio, plus informal practices.[^57]
- A pre-programme orientation of **2.5 hours** and a brief **5–10 minute individual interview**
  precede the course in the standards-of-practice format.[^56]
- Core practices: **awareness of breath, body scan, walking meditation, mindful movement, open
  awareness**.[^57]

**Agent design implication.** Quoting the real MBSR dose is useful because it sets expectations: the
trial evidence rests on roughly **20+ hours of class time and 45 min/day of practice**, not on a
10-minute app session.[^56][^57] The agent should not claim app-based micro-practices inherit MBSR's
effect sizes.[^48][^56]

## 4. Breathwork

Reference: Fincham GW, Strauss C, Montero-Marin J, Cavanagh K. *Effect of breathwork on stress and
mental health: a meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials.* Sci Rep. 2023 Jan 9;13(1):432.[^52]

- Primary outcome was self-reported stress versus non-breathwork controls.[^52]
- **Anxiety (k = 20): g = −0.32, p < 0.0001.**[^52]
- **Depressive symptoms (k = 18): g = −0.40, p < 0.0001.**[^52]
- Overall conclusion: breathwork **may be effective** for improving stress and mental health.[^52]
- The outcomes are **self-reported/subjective**, which is the main limitation to disclose.[^52]

**Agent design implication.** Breathwork is a reasonable, low-cost, low-risk tool to offer (slow
paced breathing, extended exhale) with a small-to-moderate effect size honestly stated.[^52] High-
intensity or hyperventilatory protocols are a different matter and sit outside what a fitness agent
should coach without supervision, given the meditation adverse-event literature.[^51]

## 5. Yoga

Reference: NCCIH, *Yoga: What You Need To Know* (page last updated August 2023).[^49]

- **Low-back pain**: yoga is **slightly better than no exercise**, though the differences may be
  clinically small.[^49]
- **Neck pain**: reduced pain intensity and decreased disability.[^49]
- **Headaches**: evidence suggests reduced frequency and intensity, particularly for tension
  headaches.[^49]
- **Knee osteoarthritis**: may improve pain, function and stiffness.[^49]
- **Anxiety**: yoga had a **substantial beneficial effect** across diverse populations.[^49]
- **Depression**: helpful for reducing symptoms **when combined with other treatments**.[^49]
- **PTSD**: only **low-quality evidence of a possible benefit**.[^49]
- **Sleep**: improvements documented in cancer patients, people with arthritis, and older
  adults.[^49]
- **Balance**: **11 of 15 studies** showed improvement in balance outcomes.[^49]
- **Menopause**: at least as effective as other types of exercise.[^49]
- **Smoking cessation**: participants were **37% more likely to quit** during treatment.[^49]
- In the depression network meta-analysis, yoga's effect size was **g −0.55**, second only to
  walking/jogging.[^53]

### Yoga safety

- Generally considered safe for healthy people **when performed properly under a qualified
  instructor**.[^49]
- Commonest injuries are **sprains and strains**, most often of the **knee or lower leg**; serious
  injuries are rare.[^49]
- Injury risk is **lower than for higher-impact activities**, but **emergency-department injury rates
  are higher in people aged 65+** than in younger adults.[^49]
- **Pregnancy**: avoid **hot yoga** (overheating risk) and avoid **prolonged supine positioning**.[^49]
- Beginners should avoid **extreme poses** such as headstands and lotus position.[^49]
- Modification is needed for **pre-existing injuries, spinal disease, hypertension, balance problems
  and glaucoma**.[^49]
- Pregnancy-specific corroboration: women who experience light-headedness, nausea or feeling unwell
  exercising supine should **modify the position**.[^62]

## 6. Tai chi and qigong

Reference: NCCIH, *Tai Chi: What You Need To Know* (page last updated December 2023).[^50]

- **Falls in older adults**: **low-certainty** evidence tai chi may reduce the **rate of falls by
  19%**, and **high-certainty** evidence it may reduce the **number of people who fall by
  20%**.[^50]
- **Parkinson's disease**: significant positive effect on reducing falls versus no
  intervention.[^50]
- **Stroke**: improved walking patterns short-term; effects on balance unclear.[^50]
- **Osteoarthritis**: improved pain and stiffness; the **American College of Rheumatology strongly
  recommends tai chi** for knee and hip osteoarthritis.[^50]
- Knee OA evidence strength is **low-to-moderate** for pain, stiffness and physical function.[^50]
- **Fibromyalgia**: beneficial for pain scores, with improved sleep quality, fatigue, depression and
  quality of life.[^50]
- **Low-back pain**: may decrease pain intensity and improve everyday function, alone or with physical
  therapy.[^50]
- **Blood pressure**: better at lowering systolic and diastolic blood pressure than education, other
  exercises, or medications in the cited comparison.[^50]
- **COPD**: may improve exercise capacity, lung function and quality of life.[^50]
- **Cognition**: cognitively enhanced tai chi improved working memory and executive function; in mild
  cognitive impairment, short-term benefits were **similar to other types of exercise**.[^50]
- **Depression/anxiety**: a **small** positive effect on depressive symptoms in older adults with
  chronic conditions.[^50]
- **Safety**: a 2019 review found adverse-event frequency **similar** for tai chi, another active
  intervention, or no intervention; reported events were minor, such as musculoskeletal
  discomfort.[^50]
- **Credentialing reality**: tai chi instructors **do not have to be licensed**, and there is **no
  national certification standard**.[^50]
- **Qigong** in fibromyalgia: **30–40 minutes daily for 6–8 weeks** produced consistent benefits in
  pain, sleep, and physical and mental function, still present **4–6 months** after study end.[^59]
- Meditative movement therapies (tai chi, qigong, yoga) produced **modest improvements** in sleep
  disturbance, fatigue, depressed mood and health-related quality of life, with continued improvement
  in sleep and quality of life at **3–6 months**.[^59]

**Agent design implication.** Tai chi is the agent's best-supported recommendation for **balance and
fall prevention in older adults**, and the fall-reduction figure is one of the few high-certainty
numbers in this whole file.[^50] It also satisfies the WHO older-adult balance recommendation and
ACSM's neuromotor component.[^2][^4]

## 7. The mental-health escalation line — mandatory behaviour

This section defines what the agent must do, not what it may say.

**The agent is not a mental-health provider.** It may teach general stress-management and mind-body
practice; it may not assess, diagnose, or treat mental illness, and it must not present meditation,
breathwork or exercise as a substitute for care.[^48]

**Escalate immediately, stop coaching, and surface crisis resources when any of the following
appears:**

- Any mention of **suicidal ideation, self-harm, or intent to harm others**. Suicidal ideation is a
  documented meditation adverse event, so this applies inside meditation conversations too.[^51]
- **Acute crisis or acute distress** of any kind.[^58]
- **Psychotic symptoms, dissociation, derealisation, or depersonalisation** arising during or after
  practice — cognitive anomalies were reported in 25% of adverse-event studies.[^51]
- **Trauma history surfacing** during body-scan or breath practice. Yoga and meditation for PTSD rest
  on **low-quality evidence**, which is not a basis for an AI to lead trauma-focused
  practice.[^48][^49]
- **Worsening anxiety or depression attributable to practice** — the two commonest reported adverse
  effects, at 33% and 27% of adverse-event studies respectively.[^51]
- **Disordered-eating signals** alongside exercise questions. Compulsive exercise and restriction are
  clinical matters, and NCCIH's standing caution against using these practices to postpone seeing a
  provider applies directly.[^48] Hand the nutrition half to the nutritionist agent.

**The US resource to surface:**

- The **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — **call or text 988**, or chat at **988lifeline.org** — gives
  24/7 access to trained crisis counsellors for mental-health-related distress.[^58]
- It is a network of **over 200 locally operated crisis centres** and serves as a universal entry
  point anywhere in the US.[^58]
- It offers **Spanish-language** services, services for people who are **Deaf or Hard of Hearing**,
  and connects **veterans, service members and their families** to the Veterans Crisis Line.[^58]
- It accepts contacts from **anyone who is suicidal or in emotional distress, including substance-use
  crisis**, and from people worried about someone else.[^58]

**Required disclosures when offering meditation or breathwork:**

1. State that adverse effects are documented at roughly **8% of participants** in one review of 83
   studies — comparable to psychological therapies, not zero.[^48]
2. State that the commonest are **anxiety and depression**, and that they can occur in people with no
   prior mental-health history.[^51]
3. Tell the person to **stop and seek a qualified teacher or clinician** if practice consistently
   worsens how they feel.[^48][^51]
4. Never describe meditation or breathwork as a reason to delay seeing a provider.[^48]
