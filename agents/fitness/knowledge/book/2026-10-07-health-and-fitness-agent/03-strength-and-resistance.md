## 1. The current position stand — read this one first

ACSM replaced its 2009 resistance-training position stand in 2026, so the agent's defaults should come
from the new one.[^6][^9]

- Citation [^6]: Currier BS, D'Souza AC, Fiatarone Singh MA, Lowisz CV, Rawson ES, Schoenfeld BJ,
  Smith-Ryan AE, Steen JP, Thomas GA, Triplett NT, Washington TA, Werner TJ, Phillips SM. *American
  College of Sports Medicine Position Stand. Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function,
  Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews.* Med Sci Sports
  Exerc. 2026 Apr 1;58(4):851–872. Epub 2026 Mar 5. PMID 41843416, doi 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897.[^6]
- It is an **umbrella review of 137 systematic reviews** covering **more than 30,000
  participants**, chaired by Stuart M. Phillips.[^6][^7]
- It was released publicly on **16 March 2026** and is the **first major update in 17 years**.[^9]
- Outcomes covered: hypertrophy, strength, power, endurance, contraction velocity, and physical
  function including **gait speed, balance and stair climbing**.[^6]

### The headline numbers

| Goal | Prescription | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency (all goals) | train **all major muscle groups at least twice a week** — the primary driver for hypertrophy and functional strength[^7][^8] | ACSM 2026 |
| Strength | **≥80% 1RM**, **2–3 sets per exercise**[^7][^8] | ACSM 2026 |
| Hypertrophy | **~10 sets per muscle group per week**[^7][^8] | ACSM 2026 |
| Power | **30–70% 1RM**, moving the load **as fast as possible** on the concentric phase[^7] | ACSM 2026 |

### What the 2026 stand explicitly de-emphasises

- **Training to failure is optional.** Advanced techniques including training to fatigue or momentary
  muscular failure **did not consistently change outcomes** for the average healthy adult.[^7]
- **Equipment barely matters.** Elastic bands, bodyweight exercises and home routines yield marked
  benefits; a traditional gym is not required.[^7][^9]
- **Beginners do not need a different system.** The stand rejects the idea that novices require
  categorically different programmes from more advanced trainees.[^7]
- **Complexity is not the lever.** The largest, most meaningful gain comes from the shift **from no
  resistance training to any resistance training**; consistency outranks programme
  sophistication.[^7][^8][^9]
- Programmes should move away from one-size-fits-all prescriptions and be individualised on goals,
  enjoyment and safety **to maximise long-term adherence**.[^7]

**Agent design implication.** The agent's default resistance prescription for a general-population
adult is: all major muscle groups, twice weekly, 2–3 sets per exercise, load heavy enough to be
challenging, stopping short of failure, with whatever equipment the person will actually use.[^6][^7]

## 2. The 2009 position stand — still the source of the granular numbers

The 2026 stand is an overview of reviews and is deliberately simpler. Where a user needs
goal-specific detail, the 2009 stand (*Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy
Adults*, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2009;41:687–708) remains the most granular published ACSM table.[^5]

| Goal / level | Load | Reps | Sets | Rest | Velocity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Novice & intermediate strength | **70–85% 1RM**[^5] | **8–12**[^5] | **1–3 per exercise**[^5] | — | moderate |
| Advanced strength | **70–100% 1RM**, emphasis on **1–6RM** heavy loading[^5] | 1–6[^5] | multiple[^5] | **3–5 min**[^5] | **1–2 s concentric, 1–2 s eccentric**[^5] |
| Local muscular endurance | **40–60% 1RM**[^5] | **9–15 (high reps)**[^5] | multiple[^5] | **≤90 s**[^5] | — |
| Power | **0–60% 1RM lower body; 30–60% upper body**[^5] | low[^5] | multiple[^5] | **3–5 min**[^5] | **fast**[^5] |

- **Progression rule (the one to memorise):** increase load by **2–10%** once the person can perform
  the current workload for **1–2 repetitions beyond target on two consecutive sessions**.[^5]
- Power progression uses two strategies in combination: a strength-training component **and** a
  light-load, high-velocity component.[^5]
- Where 2009 and 2026 conflict (notably on whether novices need distinct programming, and on how much
  loading precision matters), **treat 2026 as current**.[^5][^6][^7]

## 3. Movement patterns and exercise selection

The guidelines specify *muscle groups*, not exercises. A pattern-based template satisfies "all major
muscle groups" without the agent having to pick a brand of programme.

- The requirement in both guideline documents is **all major muscle groups on ≥2 days/week**.[^1][^2]
- ACSM's 2011 stand lists resistance exercise for **each major muscle group on 2–3
  days/week**.[^4]
- CDC enumerates the groups to cover: **legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms**.[^60]
- A practical six-pattern template that covers that list: **squat** (legs/hips), **hinge**
  (hips/back), **horizontal push** (chest/shoulders/arms), **horizontal pull** (back/arms),
  **vertical push or pull** (shoulders/back), **core/anti-extension** (abdomen).[^60]
- Multijoint exercises are the recommended backbone: the NSCA older-adult statement prescribes **1–2
  multijoint exercises per major muscle group**.[^30][^31]
- Because equipment is not a determinant of outcome, the same pattern template can be run with bands,
  bodyweight, dumbbells or machines.[^7][^9]

## 4. Weekly volume and frequency, reconciled

- **Frequency**: twice weekly per muscle group is the primary driver for hypertrophy and functional
  strength.[^7][^8]
- **Volume for hypertrophy**: about **10 sets per muscle group per week**.[^7][^8]
- **Volume for strength**: **2–3 sets per exercise** at **≥80% 1RM**.[^7][^8]
- **Health-outcome volume**: only **30–60 min/week** of muscle-strengthening activity is associated
  with a **10–20% lower** risk of all-cause, cardiovascular, diabetes and cancer mortality,
  independent of aerobic activity.[^13]
- That association follows a **J-shaped curve**, with no conclusive additional mortality benefit
  beyond about an hour a week.[^13]

**Agent design implication.** There are two different questions hiding in "how much lifting should I
do?" — a *health* dose (30–60 min/week, tiny) and a *hypertrophy* dose (~10 sets/muscle/week, much
larger). The agent should ask which one the person means.[^7][^13]

## 5. Technique, safety and the blood-pressure question

- A properly designed resistance programme with **appropriate instruction in technique and proper
  spotting** is safe for healthy older adults — the NSCA position statement treats instruction and
  spotting as part of the prescription, not an optional extra.[^30][^31]
- Particular attention is owed to the **shoulder complex, knee, hip and spine**.[^31]
- **Uncontrolled hypertension is a contraindication** to resistance training; resistance training is
  generally safe once blood pressure is controlled.[^31]
- Resting blood pressure **>200/>110 mmHg** is listed as a relative contraindication to exercise
  testing, which is the nearest formal threshold the agent can quote.[^68]
- Clients with **unstable medical conditions** need physician consultation before resistance
  training.[^31]
- Power exercises require careful teaching and standard safety protocols because of the high
  velocities involved.[^31]
- Intensity and volume should be modified for clients with osteoarthritis.[^31]

**Breath-holding.** The agent should not coach the Valsalva manoeuvre. It is a technique used by
experienced, well-trained lifters under heavy structural loads, and its interaction with the
blood-pressure contraindication above makes it a poor default for a general-population client being
advised remotely.[^31][^68] The safe general cue is to **avoid breath-holding** and keep breathing
through the repetition.[^31]

## 6. Older adults

NSCA position statement: Fragala MS, Cadore EL, Dorgo S, Izquierdo M, Kraemer WJ, Peterson MD, Ryan
ED. *Resistance Training for Older Adults: Position Statement From the National Strength and
Conditioning Association.* J Strength Cond Res. 2019 Aug;33(8):2019–2052.[^30]

### Healthy older adults

- **2–3 sets per major muscle group**, **2 minutes rest between sets**, on **2–3 days/week**; entry-level
  participants can start with single sets and see equivalent results.[^31]
- **Reps**: beginners **10–15**; experienced **6–12** with variation for strength development.[^31]
- **Load progression**: start **<51% 1RM**, advance to **51–69% 1RM**, and eventually **70–85% 1RM**
  for some clients.[^31]
- The headline prescription in the published statement is **2–3 sets of 1–2 multijoint exercises per
  major muscle group at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3 times per week**, plus power work.[^30]
- **Power work at 40–60% 1RM**, performed explosively on the concentric phase, to support functional
  daily activities.[^30][^31]
- **Training to failure has not been shown to add any physiological adaptation in older
  people**; for many older clients **50–70% 1RM with excellent form** is a more than satisfactory and
  safer stimulus.[^30][^31]

### Frailty

- **Three sessions/week**, progressing to **3 sets of 8–12 repetitions**.[^31]
- Load from **20–30% 1RM**, potentially advancing toward **80% 1RM** over time.[^31]
- Power exercises at **30–60% 1RM**.[^31]
- Include **sit-to-stand** and other movements that simulate daily activities.[^31]

### Why it matters

- WHO adds a specific older-adult recommendation: **multicomponent activity emphasising balance and
  strength on ≥3 days/week**, on strong recommendation and high-to-moderate certainty.[^2]
- CDC frames the same thing as aerobic + muscle-strengthening (**≥2 days**) + **balance** activity
  each week.[^60]
- The 2026 ACSM stand explicitly includes **gait speed, balance and stair climbing** among the
  physical-function outcomes resistance training improves.[^6]

## 7. Youth

NSCA's *Youth Resistance Training: Updated Position Statement Paper* (2009) is the reference.[^32]

- A properly designed and supervised youth resistance programme is **relatively safe for
  youth**.[^32]
- It can enhance **muscular strength and power**, improve the **cardiovascular risk profile**, and
  improve **motor skill performance**.[^32]
- It can **increase a young athlete's resistance to sports-related injuries** and improve
  **psychosocial well-being**.[^32]
- It can help establish exercise habits during childhood and adolescence.[^32]
- Instruction and supervision must come from **qualified adults** who understand both youth
  resistance-training guidelines and the physical and psychosocial particulars of children and
  adolescents.[^32]
- Injury risk is minimised by **qualified supervision, appropriate programme design, sensible
  progression and careful equipment selection**.[^32]
- The guideline-level requirement for youth 6–17 is **60 min/day MVPA** including muscle- and
  bone-strengthening activity; WHO specifies muscle/bone strengthening on **≥3 days/week**.[^1][^2]

**Agent design implication.** The agent can affirm that supervised youth strength training is safe and
beneficial — contradicting the persistent "it stunts growth" belief — while insisting on qualified
in-person supervision rather than remote programming for minors.[^32]

## 8. Resistance training for specific outcomes

- **Blood pressure**: in a network meta-analysis of **270 RCTs and 15,827 participants** (Edwards et
  al., Br J Sports Med 2023;57:1317–26), **isometric** exercise produced the largest resting
  blood-pressure reductions of any mode — **−8.24 mmHg systolic, −4.00 mmHg diastolic**.[^33]
- Among isometric sub-modes, the **wall squat ranked highest** for systolic reduction, with the
  largest effects in people with elevated baseline values.[^33]
- **Depression**: strength training had an antidepressant effect size of **Hedges' g −0.49** versus
  active controls in a 218-study network meta-analysis — comparable to CBT (g −0.55) in that same
  analysis.[^53]
- **Mortality**: 30–60 min/week of muscle strengthening, **10–20% lower** risk across major
  non-communicable disease outcomes.[^13]
- **Type 2 diabetes**: ACSM's 2022 consensus statement covers resistance training alongside aerobic
  work and adds reducing sedentary time and breaking up sitting.[^69]

**Caution on the isometric finding.** It is a single network meta-analysis, and isometric holds raise
blood pressure acutely and interact with the breath-holding caution above, so the agent should not
recommend wall squats to someone with uncontrolled hypertension without clinical
clearance.[^31][^33][^68]
