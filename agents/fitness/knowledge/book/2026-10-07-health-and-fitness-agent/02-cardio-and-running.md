# Cardio, Aerobic Modalities and Running

> Scope note: training-side only. Fuelling, hydration volumes and carbohydrate strategy belong to the
> sibling nutritionist agent (see `07-nutrition-interface-boundary-note.md`).

---

## 1. The weekly aerobic target, restated

- **150–300 min/week moderate** or **75–150 min/week vigorous**, or an equivalent mix, is the adult
  target in both the US and WHO documents.[^1][^2]
- ACSM's operational split: **≥30 min on ≥5 days** moderate, or **≥20 min on ≥3 days**
  vigorous.[^4]
- No minimum bout length applies any more — short bouts count toward the weekly total.[^1]
- Going beyond 300 min/week moderate is a *conditional* recommendation for extra benefit, not a
  requirement.[^2]

## 2. Intensity zones the agent can actually prescribe

| Zone label | Anchor | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Light | < 64% HRmax[^16] | recovery, NEAT, sitting-break walks[^17] |
| Moderate ("Zone 2"-ish) | 64–76% HRmax; talk-but-not-sing[^15][^16] | the bulk of weekly volume[^23] |
| Vigorous | 77–93% HRmax; only a few words between breaths[^15][^16] | intervals, tempo, threshold work[^23] |

- Estimated HRmax is `220 − age`; a 25-year-old's moderate band is roughly **125–148 bpm**.[^16]
- Metabolic anchors: moderate is **3–5.9 METs**, vigorous is **≥6.0 METs**.[^3]
- RPE anchors on the Borg 6–20 scale: moderate ≈ **12–13**, vigorous ≈ **≥14**.[^3]

### Zone 2: what it is, and the honest caveat

- Zone 2 is conventionally placed at or just below the **first lactate threshold (LT1)** —
  commonly operationalised as blood lactate below about **2.0 mmol/L**.[^22]
- Markers actually used in the literature include **72–82% HRmax** (Norwegian Olympic Federation),
  **1.5–2.5 mmol/L lactate**, **baseline lactate + 0.5 mmol/L**, **ventilatory threshold 1**, **65%
  of peak VO2**, and **maximal fat-oxidation intensity (FatMax)**.[^22]
- In 50 experienced cyclists, these markers did **not agree with each other**: coefficients of
  variation ran from **6% to 29%**, highest for power at 1.5 mmol/L (CV 29.0%) and lowest for 82%
  HRmax (CV 6.0%).[^22]
- The authors' conclusion is that commonly used percentage-based markers **may not produce consistent
  metabolic responses across individuals**, and that a fixed percentage can land an individual too
  low or too high.[^22]

**Agent design implication.** The agent should present Zone 2 as "easy, conversational, sustainable
for an hour" and treat any HR percentage as an estimate — not tell a user their watch's Zone 2 is
physiologically correct for them.[^22]

## 3. Steady-state vs interval training

### HIIT vs moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT)

- A dose-response meta-analysis found HIIT has a **non-linear** relationship between session duration
  and VO2max gain: **~11 min/session** achieved 80% of the maximal observed effect
  (**ΔVO2max ≈ 3.45 mL/kg/min**).[^20]
- MICT showed a **linear** dose-response, needing **~52 min/session** to reach 80% of its maximal
  effect — four to five times longer than HIIT for a comparable response.[^20]
- In patients with cardiovascular disease, HIIT increased VO2peak **more than MICT**, with larger
  gains for medium-length intervals and programmes longer than **12 weeks**.[^21]
- HIIT was also found **safe** in that cardiac population.[^21]
- For broader cardiometabolic risk factors, HIIT and MICT were **comparable**.[^21]

**Agent design implication.** The defensible claim is time-efficiency for cardiorespiratory fitness,
not superiority across the board.[^20][^21] HIIT also carries higher perceived exertion and higher
musculoskeletal and cardiac acute risk, so it should be gated behind screening and a base of
moderate work (see `06-special-populations-and-safety.md`).[^67]

### Polarized / 80-20 distribution

- Intensity zones in this literature: **LIT** below the first ventilatory threshold (<2 mmol/L),
  **MIT** between the thresholds (2–4 mmol/L), **HIT** above the second threshold (>4 mmol/L).[^23]
- The polarized model is approximately **70–75% LIT, 0–5% MIT, 15–20% HIT** by volume.[^23]
- In a systematic review of **14 studies (1,836 screened, 163 participants total)**, 8 of 10 studies
  measuring VO2max/VO2peak showed improvement and **5 reached statistical significance**.[^23]
- **Significant work-economy improvements appeared in 5 studies**, with small-to-moderate effect
  sizes.[^23]
- Stated limitations: small total sample, possible publication bias, and only **short-term** effects
  examined — long-term maintenance is ambiguous.[^23]

**Agent design implication.** "Most of your running easy, a small slice hard" is well supported as a
structure for trained endurance athletes.[^23] For a general-population client doing 150 min/week it
is over-engineering; the weekly guideline target already implies mostly moderate work.[^1][^2]

### Other named methods

- **Tempo / threshold work** sits in the MIT band between the ventilatory thresholds (roughly 2–4
  mmol/L lactate) — the zone the polarized model deliberately minimises.[^23]
- **Fartlek** is unstructured alternation of faster and easier running; it maps onto the same
  intensity bands and is best described to a client via the talk test rather than pace.[^15]
- **Interval prescription via FITT** is explicitly supported: frequency, interval intensity, interval
  and recovery duration, and mode are all prescribable variables.[^3]

## 4. The "fat-burning zone"

- Fat oxidation rises with intensity to a maximum (**FatMax**) at roughly **60–65% VO2max**, then
  falls steeply as intensity increases further — the "crossover" point.[^27]
- Typical maximal fat-oxidation rates are **0.3–0.6 g/min**, with large between-individual variation
  even in homogeneous groups.[^27]
- Fat oxidation is minimal at **≥85% VO2max**, where carbohydrate dominates.[^27]
- Around **70% HRmax** is where fat is burned at the highest rate per minute — but this framing
  overlooks that higher-intensity work burns **more total calories**, so the proportion of fat used
  during the session is not the determinant of fat loss.[^16]

**Agent design implication.** The physiology is real; the inference is not. The agent should say that
the "fat-burning zone" describes fuel *mix*, not fat *loss*, and should hand off anything about
energy balance, deficits or body-composition targets to the nutritionist agent.[^16][^27]

## 5. Running: volume, progression and injury

### The 10% rule is weaker than its reputation — and the real signal is per-session

The best current evidence is the **Garmin-RUNSAFE Running Health Study** (Br J Sports Med 2025;
59(17):1203–1210; doi 10.1136/bjsports-2024-109380).[^24]

- **5,205 runners** (mean age 45.8, SD 10.4; 22% female) were followed; **1,820 (35%) sustained a
  running-related injury** across **588,071 sessions**.[^24]
- Overuse-injury rate rose significantly when a **single session exceeded 110% of the longest run in
  the previous 30 days**.[^24]
- Hazard rate ratios vs. staying at or under +10%: **small spike (>10–30%) HRR 1.64**; **moderate
  spike (>30–100%) HRR 1.52**; **large spike (>100%) HRR 2.28**.[^24]
- By contrast, the **acute:chronic workload ratio showed a negative dose-response**, and the
  **week-to-week ratio showed no relationship** with injury.[^24]
- Earlier evidence already undercut the weekly 10% rule: a 13-week programme averaging **+10%/week**
  showed no significant difference in running-related injury incidence versus an 8-week programme
  averaging **+24%/week**.[^25]

**Agent design implication.** Replace "increase weekly mileage by no more than 10%" with "keep any
single run within about 10% of your longest run in the last month," which is what the data actually
supports.[^24][^25]

### Other RUNSAFE findings on self-reported training characteristics

- The Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort has also been used to examine which **self-reported training
  characteristics** best identify who is more likely to sustain running-related injuries.[^29]
- That companion analysis is the right source to cite when a user asks whether their own reported
  habits are a risk factor, rather than generalising from the single-session spike finding.[^29]

### Strength training is the strongest injury-prevention lever

- Lauersen et al. (*BJSM* 2014) meta-analysed randomised controlled trials of exercise interventions
  for sports-injury prevention.[^26]
- **Strength training** reduced sports injuries to **less than one third** of control levels, and
  reduced **overuse injuries by almost half**.[^26]
- **Proprioceptive training** reduced injury risk by roughly **45%**.[^26]
- **Stretching showed no significant injury-prevention benefit**.[^26]
- Multiple-exposure programmes also reduced injuries, but by less than strength training
  alone.[^26]

**Agent design implication.** For a runner asking how to stay healthy, the first answer is two
strength sessions a week and conservative single-session jumps — not stretching.[^24][^26]

### Training errors and the broader risk picture

- A systematic review of training errors and running-related injuries found training parameters are
  consistently implicated, but the evidence base at that time was limited and heterogeneous.[^25]
- Biomechanical risk factors for overuse injury in distance runners have been reviewed with attention
  to injury specificity; the review calls for injury-specific rather than pooled analyses.[^28]
- That matters for the agent because it means no single gait or cadence cue can be offered as a
  general injury-prevention measure.[^28]

## 6. A defensible couch-to-5k style progression

Construct it from the primitives above rather than from a branded plan, and say plainly that it is a
template rather than a prescription for a specific person.[^1][^64]

- **Weeks 1–3, establish frequency.** 3 sessions/week of walk/run intervals, total session 20–30
  min, run segments at talk-test moderate (can talk, not sing).[^15] Frequency first because the
  guideline target is a weekly volume, not a single long effort.[^1]
- **Progression rule.** Lengthen the run segments so no single session exceeds about **110% of the
  longest continuous run in the previous 30 days**.[^24]
- **Strength, from week 1.** Two sessions/week covering hips, glutes, quadriceps and calves —
  strength training is the single best-supported injury-reduction intervention.[^26]
- **Weeks 4–8, consolidate.** Keep 3 runs/week; one can grow toward 30 continuous minutes while the
  other two stay short. Total weekly moderate minutes should pass **150** around here, which is the
  health target itself.[^1][^2]
- **Weeks 9–12, add one faster session.** A single weekly vigorous session (intervals or tempo) is
  enough to collect the VO2max benefit efficiently — roughly **11 minutes of interval work per
  session** captures 80% of the achievable effect.[^20]
- **Do not add static stretching as a warm-up fix.** It does not reduce injury risk,[^26] and long
  holds impair the next effort (see `04-mobility-flexibility-recovery.md`).[^35]
- **Deload / step-back weeks.** Reduce volume rather than stopping; strength is not meaningfully lost
  in under 7 days of reduced training.[^46]

## 7. Cardio for specific health outcomes (training-side)

- In cardiac rehabilitation populations, HIIT raised VO2peak more than MICT and was safe, with
  programmes **longer than 12 weeks** showing the larger effects.[^21]
- Aerobic training improves glycaemic management in adults with type 2 diabetes, with less daily time
  in hyperglycaemia (ACSM consensus statement, 2022).[^69]
- Across 270 randomised trials and 15,827 participants, **isometric** exercise produced the largest
  resting blood-pressure reductions of any modality (**−8.24 mmHg systolic, −4.00 mmHg
  diastolic**), with the **wall squat** ranking highest among isometric sub-modes.[^33]
- Walking/jogging had the largest antidepressant effect size of any exercise modality in a network
  meta-analysis of 218 studies (**Hedges' g −0.63**) — covered in
  `05-mind-body-mental-wellness.md`.[^53]
