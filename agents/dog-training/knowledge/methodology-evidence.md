# The studies behind the method, with their limits

## Study by study
- Herron, Shofer and Reisner 2009 (owner survey, referral dogs): Aggressive responses to hitting or kicking 43%, growling at the dog 41%, forcing release of an item 39%, alpha roll 31%. Limit: retrospective owner report. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:97
- Herron 2009, same survey: Reward techniques drew owner-directed aggression in 0% (clicker, "look at me") to 6% (trading food for an object). @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:98
- Ziv 2017 (review of 17 studies): Aversive methods can jeopardise physical and mental health; reward-based recommended first line. Limit: mixed underlying studies. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:100
- Cooper et al. 2014 (63 dogs, recall problems): E-collar training caused signs of distress, especially at high settings, with no consistent benefit. Limit: short, referral population. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:101
- Vieira de Castro et al. 2020 (92 dogs): Aversive group showed far more lip licking (55.90 against 4.11 events), a higher cortisol rise and slower cognitive-bias responses. Limit: not randomised. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:102
- China, Mills and Cooper 2020 (63 dogs, off-lead problems): Reward-based training gave significantly better outcomes; no evidence e-collars are needed even for recall. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:104
- Johnson and Wynne 2024 (19 dogs, one lure-chasing task): E-collar group stopped chasing within 1 to 2 sessions; food groups failed all four tests. Small sample, no follow-up, and a published critique says its welfare measures cannot support a welfare-neutral conclusion. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:106
- Rooney and Cowan 2011: Reward use went with better novel-task performance; punishment with less playful dogs. Limit: correlational. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:108
- Learning and Behavior 2024 (conceptual paper): Asks what evidence could validate a training method at all, since the field rests largely on owner surveys. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:109

## When someone asks "which method is right?"
- State the default and whose standard it is: reward-based, per AVSAB 2021 and the 2024 four-federation position. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:170
- Give the strongest support with its numbers: Vieira de Castro 2020 on welfare, China 2020 on recall. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:172
- Give the strongest contrary evidence with its numbers and limits: Johnson and Wynne 2024, 19 dogs, one task, with the published critique on the same line. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:174
- Name the institutional dissent: IACP, on tool access and legislation. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:176
- State the risk concretely: confrontational handling drew owner-directed aggression in up to 43% of cases, against 0 to 6% for reward techniques. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:177
- Then recommend, rather than leaving the handler to choose blind. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:179

## Markers and clickers
- Chiandetti et al. (2016): food alone, food plus clicker, and food plus a spoken word all taught a new behaviour equally well. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:118
- Feng, Howell and Bennett (2016) called the overall findings surprisingly inconclusive. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:121
- So teach marker timing, because precise reward delivery is the mechanism, and never claim a clicker beats a verbal marker. @2026-10-07-dog-training-coach-agent/04-training-methodology.md:129
