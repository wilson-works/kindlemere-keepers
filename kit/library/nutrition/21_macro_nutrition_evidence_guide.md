# The evidence-based macro nutrition guide for every goal

**Setting your macros correctly is the single highest-leverage decision in any body composition plan.** Whether you're cutting fat, maintaining, or building muscle, the fundamentals are identical: calculate your energy needs, set protein first, assign fat minimums, fill the rest with carbs, then adjust every few weeks based on real data. This guide distills the current evidence — from ISSN position stands, peer-reviewed research by Helms, Aragon, and Trexler, and the landmark MATADOR study — into a complete, actionable system. Every number below is grounded in published research, not guesswork.

---

## How to calculate your calorie target from scratch

The process follows three steps: estimate your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), multiply by an activity factor to get Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE), then adjust for your goal.

### Step 1: The Mifflin-St Jeor equation

The **Mifflin-St Jeor formula** is the gold standard for estimating BMR. A systematic review by Frankenfield et al. (2005) found it predicted BMR within 10% of measured values in 82% of non-obese individuals — outperforming every other common equation, including Harris-Benedict.

- **Men:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
- **Women:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

For imperial users: divide pounds by 2.205 for kilograms; multiply inches by 2.54 for centimeters.

### Step 2: Activity multipliers

Multiply your BMR by the factor that best matches your overall daily activity level — not just your gym time. People with desk jobs who train 3–4 times per week typically fall into "lightly active," not "moderately active." Overestimating activity level is the most common TDEE calculation error.

| Activity level | Multiplier | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary | **1.2** | Desk job, little to no exercise |
| Lightly active | **1.375** | Light exercise 1–3 days/week |
| Moderately active | **1.55** | Moderate exercise 3–5 days/week + active lifestyle |
| Very active | **1.725** | Hard exercise 6–7 days/week |
| Extremely active | **1.9** | Two-a-day training or heavy physical labor |

### Step 3: Set your calorie target by goal

| Goal | Calorie adjustment | Expected rate of change |
|---|---|---|
| **Fat loss** | TDEE minus 20–25% (or ~500 kcal/day) | 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week |
| **Maintenance** | Eat at TDEE | Weight stable within ±1–2 lbs |
| **Muscle gain** | TDEE plus 200–350 kcal/day | 1–3 lbs/month (varies by training experience) |

For fat loss, leaner individuals should use a smaller deficit (closer to 15–20%) and target the lower end of the weight-loss range to preserve muscle. Garthe et al. (2013) demonstrated that surpluses exceeding **500 kcal/day** produce significantly more fat gain without additional muscle growth, so conservative surpluses are strongly preferred for lean bulking.

## Setting macros: protein first, then fat, then carbs

The evidence-based approach treats carbohydrates as the "flex macro" — you set protein and fat floors first, then fill remaining calories with carbs. This is the consensus method used by virtually every credible sports nutrition researcher.

### Macro targets by goal

| Macro | Fat loss | Maintenance | Muscle gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protein** | **1.0–1.3 g/lb** bodyweight | 0.8–1.0 g/lb | 0.8–1.0 g/lb |
| **Fat** | 0.3–0.4 g/lb (20–30% of calories) | 0.35–0.5 g/lb (25–35%) | 0.35–0.5 g/lb (25–30%) |
| **Carbs** | Remaining calories (typically 30–45%) | Remaining calories (40–55%) | Remaining calories (40–55%) |

**Protein is highest during fat loss, not muscle gain.** This counterintuitive finding is one of the most robust in sports nutrition. During caloric restriction, higher protein intake (up to **2.3–3.1 g/kg of lean body mass** per the ISSN position stand) protects against muscle loss. During a surplus, the caloric excess itself is muscle-sparing, so slightly lower protein suffices. The practical heuristic of **1 g per pound of bodyweight** works well across all goals and is the simplest target to remember.

Fat should never drop below **0.3 g/lb** (roughly 15% of calories) for extended periods. Research by Hämäläinen et al. found testosterone levels can decline by up to 25% when dietary fat falls below this threshold. Fat is essential for hormone production, fat-soluble vitamin absorption, and cell membrane integrity.

### Worked example: 180 lb male, 5'10", 30 years old, moderately active

**BMR:** (10 × 81.6) + (6.25 × 177.8) − (5 × 30) + 5 = **1,783 kcal/day**
**TDEE:** 1,783 × 1.55 = **2,764 kcal/day**

| Goal | Calories | Protein | Carbs | Fat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fat loss** | ~2,265 | 200g (35%) | 220g (39%) | 65g (26%) |
| **Maintenance** | ~2,765 | 180g (26%) | 343g (50%) | 75g (24%) |
| **Muscle gain** | ~3,065 | 180g (24%) | 406g (53%) | 80g (23%) |

## The food toolkit: what to eat for each macro

Building meals becomes straightforward once you understand which foods deliver which macros most efficiently. The lists below are organized by primary macronutrient, with nutritional data sourced from the USDA FoodData Central database.

### High-protein foods ranked by protein density

The best protein sources deliver the most protein per calorie. **Cod, chicken breast, egg whites, and canned tuna** all provide roughly **20+ grams of protein per 100 calories**, making them the workhorses of any macro-based diet. For a 6 oz cooked serving, chicken breast delivers **54g protein at 267 calories**, while cod provides **41g at just 178 calories**. Greek yogurt (nonfat, plain) offers 17–20g protein per cup at approximately 100 calories, making it one of the most versatile dairy options. Whey protein powder remains the most convenient option at roughly 24g protein per scoop and 120 calories.

Plant-based options require larger portions to match animal sources. Firm tofu provides **43g protein per cup** (363 calories), and tempeh delivers 34g per cup (319 calories). Lentils and black beans offer 15–18g protein per cooked cup but come with 40g of carbohydrates — making them excellent combination foods for those who need both macros.

### Complex carbohydrate sources

For training fuel and sustained energy, prioritize these staples: **brown rice** (52g carbs per cooked cup, 248 calories), **white rice** (45g carbs, 204 calories — a faster-digesting option ideal around training), **oats** (27g carbs per half-cup dry, 150 calories), **quinoa** (39g carbs, 222 calories — with the bonus of 8g protein), and **sweet potatoes** (41g carbs per cup, 180 calories with 6.6g fiber). Whole wheat pasta delivers 37g carbs with 6.3g fiber per cup, while fruits like bananas (27g carbs, 105 calories) serve as excellent pre-workout options.

### Healthy fat sources

The highest-quality fat sources are dominated by monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats. **Extra virgin olive oil** (14g fat per tablespoon, 73% monounsaturated) and **avocado** (15g fat per half, 67% monounsaturated) form the foundation. For omega-3 fatty acids, prioritize **salmon** (14g fat per 6 oz, rich in EPA/DHA), **walnuts** (18g fat per ounce, 72% polyunsaturated), and **chia seeds** (9g fat per ounce, 75% polyunsaturated plus 10.6g fiber). Nut butters — peanut or almond, 2 tablespoons — provide 16–18g fat alongside 7g protein, making them ideal for calorie-dense meal plans during bulking phases.

## Sample meal templates for each goal

These templates provide concrete daily blueprints. Adjust portions to match your individual macro targets from the calculations above.

### Fat loss template (~1,800 calories | 160P / 150C / 53F)

**Breakfast** (~350 cal): 1 cup egg whites scrambled with 1 whole egg, 1 slice whole wheat toast, and ½ cup blueberries. This front-loads **33g protein** while keeping fat low, leaving room for balanced meals later.

**Lunch** (~430 cal): 5 oz grilled chicken breast over ½ cup brown rice with mixed greens and ½ tablespoon olive oil vinaigrette. Delivers **49g protein** with enough carbs to sustain afternoon energy.

**Snack** (~200 cal): 1 cup nonfat Greek yogurt with ½ sliced apple and 5 almonds. The combination of casein protein, fiber, and a small amount of fat keeps hunger at bay for hours.

**Dinner** (~520 cal): 6 oz baked cod, 1 medium sweet potato, 1 cup steamed broccoli, and ¼ avocado. Cod's exceptionally high protein-to-calorie ratio is the secret weapon of every cutting diet — **41g protein at just 178 calories** for the fish alone.

**Evening snack** (~170 cal): 1 scoop whey protein in water with 1 cup strawberries. A low-calorie way to hit the protein target while satisfying a sweet craving.

### Maintenance template (~2,500 calories | 180P / 260C / 78F)

**Breakfast** (~500 cal): 1 cup rolled oats cooked with 1 scoop whey protein stirred in, topped with 1 sliced banana and 1 tablespoon almond butter. The oats-plus-whey combination delivers **33g protein with 58g carbs** and is ready in under five minutes.

**Lunch** (~700 cal): 6 oz grilled chicken breast with 1 cup brown rice, 1 cup roasted vegetables, and 1 tablespoon olive oil for cooking. A classic bodybuilding staple that hits **62g protein and 70g carbs** in a single sitting.

**Snack** (~300 cal): ½ cup low-fat cottage cheese, 1 oz almonds, and ½ cup blueberries. This combines slow-digesting casein with healthy fats and antioxidants.

**Dinner** (~675 cal): 6 oz baked salmon over 1 cup cooked quinoa with a large mixed green salad dressed with 1 tablespoon olive oil and vinegar. Salmon provides both protein and omega-3 fats, while quinoa contributes additional protein and complex carbs.

**Evening snack** (~325 cal): 1 cup nonfat Greek yogurt drizzled with 1 tablespoon honey, topped with 1 tablespoon chia seeds and a sliced apple. The chia seeds add omega-3s and fiber to an already nutrient-dense snack.

### Muscle gain template (~3,000 calories | 210P / 340C / 88F)

**Breakfast** (~775 cal): 3 whole eggs scrambled, 1 cup oatmeal, 1 medium banana, 2 slices whole wheat toast, and 1 cup 2% milk. This high-volume breakfast pushes **41g protein and 100g+ carbs** to fuel morning training.

**Lunch** (~750 cal): 6 oz grilled chicken breast with 1.5 cups white rice, 1 cup steamed broccoli, and 1 tablespoon olive oil. White rice is the preferred carbohydrate here for its digestibility and caloric density — important when total intake is high.

**Snack** (~430 cal): A protein shake blended from 1 scoop whey protein, 1 large banana, and 2 tablespoons peanut butter. This liquid meal is the easiest way to add 430 calories without suppressing appetite for dinner.

**Dinner** (~790 cal): 6 oz baked salmon, 1 large sweet potato, and 1 cup cooked quinoa with sautéed mixed vegetables. The combination of salmon's omega-3s with two carbohydrate sources ensures both recovery and glycogen replenishment.

**Evening snack** (~400 cal): 1 cup low-fat cottage cheese with 1 oz walnuts and ½ cup mixed berries. Cottage cheese's casein protein digests slowly overnight, supporting muscle protein synthesis during sleep.

## How to adjust every 2–4 weeks based on real data

No calculation is perfectly accurate on the first attempt. The initial numbers are a starting point — **systematic tracking and adjustment is where results actually come from.**

### What to track and how

**Weigh daily, but only act on weekly averages.** Daily weight fluctuates **1–3+ pounds** from water retention, sodium intake, glycogen shifts, gut content, and hormonal cycles. Research by Steinberg et al. (2015) found that daily self-weighers lost roughly three times more weight than less-frequent weighers — not because the scale itself causes fat loss, but because the data enables better decisions. Weigh each morning, fasted, after using the bathroom, on the same scale. Record the number and move on. At the end of each week, calculate the average and compare it to the previous week's average.

**Take body measurements every 2–4 weeks** at the waist (at navel), hips (widest point), chest, arms, and thighs. Use the same conditions each time — morning, relaxed posture, same anatomical landmarks. Measurements often reveal body composition changes that the scale misses entirely, particularly during recomposition.

**Photograph your physique every 2–4 weeks** under identical conditions: same lighting, same time of day, same poses (front relaxed, side, back), same camera distance. Lighting alone can dramatically alter perceived leanness, so consistency matters more than flattery.

**Track gym performance** by logging key compound lifts and total training volume. During fat loss, the primary goal is strength maintenance — declining strength over two or more consecutive weeks may signal excessive deficit or inadequate recovery.

### The adjustment decision tree

Before making any adjustment, confirm that adherence has exceeded **90%** for the previous two weeks. Most apparent plateaus are actually adherence issues. Restaurant meals contain roughly double the calories of home estimates, and even experienced trackers underreport intake by 10–20%.

**During fat loss:** If weekly average weight has not declined for two consecutive weeks despite confirmed adherence, reduce daily intake by **100–200 calories** — typically by cutting 15–25g of carbs or 5–10g of fat while keeping protein constant or slightly increasing it. Alternatively, increase daily step count by 1,500–2,000 or add one cardio session per week. Repeat this cycle as needed, always making the smallest effective change.

**During muscle gain:** If weekly weight is not trending upward after 2–3 weeks, increase intake by **100–200 calories per day**, primarily from carbohydrates. If weight is climbing faster than expected for your training level — beginners should gain roughly 2–3 lbs/month, intermediates 1–2 lbs/month, advanced trainees under 1 lb/month — reduce the surplus by a similar amount.

**At maintenance:** True maintenance exists as a **200–300 calorie range**, not a single number. Track for 2–3 weeks and adjust by 100–200 calories if weight trends consistently in either direction.

### What a real plateau looks like versus a false alarm

A **true plateau** requires all of the following for at least 2–3 consecutive weeks: no change in weekly average weight, no change in waist or other measurements, no visible change in progress photos, and no improvement in gym performance — all while maintaining greater than 90% dietary adherence. If the scale is stagnant but your waist measurement is shrinking, you are recomposing, not plateauing.

Common false alarms include water retention from a new training program (exercise-induced inflammation can mask 2–4 lbs of fat loss), high sodium intake, the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle (which can cause 2–6+ lbs of water retention), and elevated cortisol from stress or sleep deprivation. The solution is patience: maintain adherence for 2–3 full weeks before concluding the plateau is real.

## Diet breaks, refeeds, and knowing when to pull back

Extended dieting triggers **adaptive thermogenesis** — a reduction in energy expenditure beyond what's explained by lost body mass, averaging roughly **120 kcal/day** according to a 2022 review in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. This adaptation emerges after approximately two weeks of restriction and manifests primarily as reduced NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis), decreased thyroid hormone (T3), and lower leptin levels.

### Refeed days restore glycogen and sanity

A refeed day is a planned increase to approximately maintenance calories, achieved primarily by adding **50–150g of carbohydrates** while keeping protein unchanged and slightly reducing fat. Carbohydrates have a larger effect on leptin and thyroid levels than other macros. Campbell et al. (2020) found that a 2-day carbohydrate refeed during a 7-week energy restriction preserved both fat-free mass and resting metabolic rate in resistance-trained individuals compared to continuous restriction. Schedule refeeds **1–2 times per week** during a cut, increasing frequency as body fat decreases. These are not "cheat days" — they are structured, tracked increases in carbohydrate intake.

### Diet breaks are the strongest tool against metabolic slowdown

A diet break is **1–2 full weeks at maintenance calories** inserted during a fat loss phase. The landmark **MATADOR study** (Byrne et al., 2017) demonstrated their power: obese men who alternated 2-week dieting blocks with 2-week maintenance breaks lost **47% more weight** than those who dieted continuously for the same total duration of restriction — and retained that advantage at 6-month follow-up. The maintenance blocks attenuated adaptive thermogenesis, keeping metabolic rate closer to predicted levels.

Implement a diet break after **8–12 consecutive weeks** of dieting, or sooner if you experience persistent fatigue, declining gym performance, disrupted sleep, increased irritability, or loss of libido. These are signs that energy availability has dropped below sustainable levels.

### Reverse dieting after a cut

After completing a fat loss phase, gradually increase calories by **50–100 kcal per week** (primarily from carbohydrates) until weight stabilizes at your new maintenance level. A 2024 randomized controlled trial by Campbell et al. found no metabolic advantage to reverse dieting over an immediate return to maintenance — the metabolism doesn't "speed up" from slow increases. However, reverse dieting provides useful structure during the psychologically vulnerable post-diet transition and helps you precisely identify your new maintenance intake through gradual titration, rather than guessing and overshooting.

### Red flags that demand immediate attention

Stop cutting and increase calories if you experience multiple concurrent signs of undereating: persistent fatigue despite adequate sleep, strength regression lasting more than two weeks, hair loss, menstrual irregularities (in women — loss of a menstrual cycle indicates dangerously low energy availability below **30 kcal/kg of fat-free mass per day**), chronic cold intolerance, or obsessive thoughts about food. These signals indicate your deficit has exceeded your body's capacity to adapt safely.

During a bulk, watch for excessive fat gain by monitoring waist measurements. If your waist is growing significantly faster than your lifts, the surplus is too aggressive. A well-calibrated surplus produces roughly a **1:1 ratio of muscle to fat gain** — if you're gaining substantially more fat than muscle, reduce intake by 100–200 calories.

## Conclusion

The system is simpler than the fitness industry makes it appear. Calculate TDEE using Mifflin-St Jeor, set protein at approximately **1 g per pound of bodyweight** (higher during cuts, slightly lower during bulks), keep fat above **0.3 g/lb**, fill the remainder with carbohydrates, and then adjust by 100–200 calories every 2–4 weeks based on the trend in your weekly weight averages. The specific foods matter far less than hitting the macro targets consistently — though prioritizing whole foods, lean proteins, and minimally processed carbohydrate and fat sources will support both health and satiety. Use refeeds weekly during extended cuts, schedule full diet breaks every 8–12 weeks, and never sacrifice long-term hormonal health for short-term scale movement. The best diet is one you can execute at greater than 90% adherence indefinitely.
