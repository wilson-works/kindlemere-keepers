# The evidence-based guide to six fitness supplements

**Only three of the six most popular fitness supplements have robust evidence of benefit for most users: creatine, protein powder, and caffeine.** The other three — vitamin D, omega-3 fatty acids, and magnesium — deliver meaningful effects primarily for people who are deficient, which turns out to be a surprisingly large share of the population. This distinction matters because it determines whether you're investing in genuine performance or paying for expensive urine. What follows distills hundreds of meta-analyses, ISSN position stands, and systematic reviews into practical, actionable guidance for each supplement — covering what dose actually works, when to take it, which products to buy, and whether you personally should bother.

---

## Creatine: the most proven supplement in sports nutrition

Creatine monohydrate stands alone as **the single most effective legal ergogenic supplement available**, backed by over 500 peer-reviewed studies and the unequivocal endorsement of the International Society of Sports Nutrition. The evidence is not close — no other supplement matches its breadth and depth of support.

**Dosing is straightforward.** Take **3–5 g daily**, every day, indefinitely. A loading phase (20 g/day split into four doses for 5–7 days) saturates muscles in about a week rather than three to four weeks, but it's optional and can cause GI discomfort. Body-weight-based dosing of 0.3 g/kg for loading and 0.03 g/kg for maintenance is sometimes recommended, but 5 g/day works for virtually everyone. Cycling on and off is unnecessary — long-term supplementation does not downregulate endogenous creatine production, and stores deplete within two to six weeks of stopping. Safety has been confirmed at doses up to 30 g/day for five years with no clinically significant adverse effects.

**Timing barely matters.** A 2022 review in *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* concluded that evidence is "unclear as to whether timing of creatine supplementation around exercise modifies its efficacy." Once muscle stores are saturated, the timing of each individual dose becomes irrelevant. If pressed, a slight trend favors post-workout ingestion, but this finding hasn't reached statistical significance in well-controlled trials. Taking creatine with a meal containing carbohydrates and protein enhances uptake via insulin-mediated transport, particularly during the first days of loading — but a normal mixed meal suffices.

**Buy creatine monohydrate and nothing else.** A 2024 *Physiological Research* study found creatine HCl produced functionally identical results to monohydrate. Buffered creatine (Kre-Alkalyn) showed no superiority in a 36-person trial, and creatine ethyl ester was proven *inferior* — it degrades into creatinine before reaching muscle. Look for **Creapure®** on the label (German-manufactured, ≥99.8% purity) and third-party certification from NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport. Avoid liquid creatine (degrades in solution), proprietary blends that obscure dose, and any product claiming a novel form is "superior."

**Who benefits?** Strength and power athletes see the strongest effects: a 2024 meta-analysis of 23 RCTs found creatine plus resistance training increased upper-body strength by **+4.43 kg** on average. Older adults gain meaningful strength and lean mass, with particular promise for combating sarcopenia. Vegetarians and vegans, whose baseline muscle creatine stores are 20–40% lower than omnivores, respond with disproportionately large increases. Cognitive benefits appear most reliably under metabolic stress — sleep deprivation, high altitude, exhausting exercise — rather than in well-rested young adults. Pure steady-state endurance athletes and anyone taking creatine sporadically are unlikely to benefit. The hair-loss myth, based on a single 2009 study, was effectively debunked by a 2025 RCT that found no effect on DHT, testosterone, or any hair outcome. The kidney-damage myth has no support in over two decades of research in healthy individuals.

## Protein powder works, but total daily intake matters far more than the powder itself

Protein powder is a convenience tool, not a magic ingredient. The landmark Morton et al. (2018) meta-analysis of 49 studies established that protein supplementation during resistance training increases fat-free mass, but **benefits plateau at approximately 1.6 g/kg/day of total protein intake**, with an upper confidence interval of 2.2 g/kg/day. Going above this yields rapidly diminishing returns for hypertrophy, though higher intakes (2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass) become genuinely important during caloric restriction — Longland et al. (2016) showed that 2.4 g/kg/day during a 40% deficit preserved lean mass and tripled fat loss compared to 1.2 g/kg/day.

**The "anabolic window" is largely a myth.** Schoenfeld, Aragon, and Krieger's 2013 meta-analysis found that when total daily protein was statistically controlled, timing effects on hypertrophy disappeared entirely. A broader window of **4–6 hours around training** is reasonable, but obsessing over immediate post-workout shakes is unnecessary. What does matter is distributing protein across **3–5 meals per day at 0.25–0.40 g/kg per meal** (roughly 20–40 g per meal), with older adults needing the higher end due to anabolic resistance. Pre-sleep casein (30–40 g) reliably increases overnight muscle protein synthesis by ~22%, though a 2023 study found whey worked equally well.

**Form selection is mostly about tolerance and budget.** Whey concentrate (70–80% protein) and isolate (90%+) produce equivalent muscle-building outcomes when protein content is matched; isolate simply offers less lactose and fewer calories per gram. Plant-based proteins — particularly a **70:30 pea-rice blend** — match whey for muscle gains when total intake is adequate, confirmed by a 2025 meta-analysis finding negligible differences across 12 direct-comparison studies. The real quality concern is **amino acid spiking**, where manufacturers add cheap glycine, taurine, or creatine to inflate nitrogen content. Red flags include these amino acids listed individually on the label, "proprietary amino acid matrices," and pricing that seems too good to be true. The Clean Label Project found **47% of protein powders exceeded Proposition 65 thresholds** for toxic metals, with plant-based powders containing 3× more lead than whey. Choose brands with NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport certification, and prefer vanilla over chocolate flavoring (which concentrates heavy metals from cocoa processing).

Protein powder is genuinely useful for athletes in caloric restriction, older adults with reduced appetite, vegans struggling to hit leucine targets, and anyone whose schedule makes whole-food protein impractical. It is unnecessary for sedentary individuals meeting the basic 0.8 g/kg RDA through food, and no proprietary "muscle-building formula" outperforms ordinary whey or a well-constructed plant blend.

## Vitamin D: most people are insufficient, but megadosing doesn't help

Roughly **40% of the global population** has serum 25(OH)D levels below 20 ng/mL, per a 2023 pooled analysis of 7.9 million participants. In the United States, the figure approaches 65% by some NHANES estimates. This makes vitamin D deficiency extraordinarily common — but the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline delivered a surprising message: for healthy adults under 75, it now recommends *against* routine supplementation beyond the 600 IU RDA, and against routine blood testing. This is a marked shift from the previous guideline's emphasis on achieving 30+ ng/mL.

**Practical dosing sits between the conservative RDA and overzealous megadosing.** The evidence strongly supports correcting frank deficiency (<20 ng/mL) to prevent osteomalacia and secondary hyperparathyroidism. Each 1,000 IU/day of D3 raises serum levels by approximately **10 ng/mL** over several weeks. For most adults without risk factors, **1,000–2,000 IU/day of D3** maintains adequate levels without approaching the 4,000 IU tolerable upper limit. That upper limit itself is less safe than commonly assumed — a 2023 meta-analysis found doses of 3,200–4,000 IU/day doubled hypercalcemia risk, and a three-year Calgary trial showed 4,000 IU/day actually *reduced* bone mineral density at the radius. Paradoxically, high doses increased fall risk in elderly women.

**Always take vitamin D with a fat-containing meal.** This is the strongest timing evidence: a landmark study found fat co-ingestion increased absorption by **32%**, and taking it with the largest meal of the day boosted serum levels by ~50% over several months. Morning versus evening doesn't meaningfully matter, though some weak evidence suggests evening dosing may suppress melatonin. The vitamin D–K2 synergy has strong mechanistic logic — K2 activates proteins that direct calcium to bones rather than arteries — but clinical trial evidence for combined supplementation remains limited. MK-7 at 90–360 mcg/day is the preferred K2 form for those who choose to add it.

**Choose D3 (cholecalciferol), never D2.** A 2024 meta-analysis of 20 RCTs confirmed D3 raises serum levels **40% more effectively** than D2, which actually suppresses endogenous D3 levels. Oil-based softgels are slightly preferred for fat-soluble absorption. Vegans should seek lichen-derived D3, which is chemically identical to animal-sourced D3. Look for USP verification (Nature Made is the most prominent USP-verified brand) or NSF Certified for Sport.

The populations with the strongest evidence of benefit are adults over 75 (moderate-certainty evidence of reduced mortality), pregnant women (reduced preterm birth risk), people with dark skin at higher latitudes, obese individuals (who may need 2–3× standard doses), those on PPIs or diuretics, and anyone with confirmed deficiency. Athletes see no ergogenic benefit beyond correcting insufficiency. The VITAL trial of 25,871 healthy adults given 2,000 IU/day for 5.3 years found no reduction in cancer, cardiovascular events, or fractures — underscoring that supplementation beyond sufficiency does not confer additional protection.

## Omega-3s demand attention to dose, form, and your actual fish intake

The January 2025 ISSN Position Stand on omega-3s formalized what the evidence has been building toward: most athletes and Western adults are profoundly omega-3 insufficient. A study of 404 NCAA Division I football players found an average Omega-3 Index of **4.4%, with not a single player above the 8% target**. The Omega-3 Index — the percentage of EPA+DHA in red blood cell membranes — is the gold-standard biomarker, with <4% classified as high cardiovascular risk and 8–12% as optimal.

**Dose depends on your goal, but most people need more than they think.** For general health, aim for a minimum of **1,000–1,500 mg combined EPA+DHA daily** to move the Omega-3 Index toward 8% over 12 weeks. Anti-inflammatory and recovery applications call for **2,000–3,000 mg/day**, with EPA-dominant formulations preferred. Triglyceride reduction requires **3,000–4,000 mg/day**, the dose used in prescription omega-3 drugs. Standard 1,000 mg fish oil capsules deliver only ~300 mg of actual EPA+DHA (30% concentration) — most people taking one capsule daily are getting a fraction of a useful dose. The EFSA has confirmed safety up to 5 g/day EPA+DHA. The primary safety signal at high doses is a modest increase in atrial fibrillation risk (HR 1.49 at >1 g/day across a meta-analysis of 81,210 participants), which matters for those with AF history.

**Take omega-3s with a fat-containing meal — this is non-negotiable.** Absorption of the ethyl ester form jumps from ~20% on an empty stomach to ~60% with fat. Even the better-absorbed triglyceride form benefits meaningfully from co-ingestion with dietary fat. Split dosing (twice daily with meals) reduces GI side effects at higher doses and may improve absorption by avoiding digestive saturation. Timing relative to workouts is irrelevant — omega-3 benefits derive from chronic membrane incorporation over weeks to months, not acute effects.

**Form and purity are where most consumers go wrong.** Re-esterified triglyceride (rTG) form delivers **50% higher plasma EPA+DHA** than ethyl ester form over two weeks. Always check the Supplement Facts panel for the actual EPA and DHA breakdown per serving — the front-label "1,000 mg fish oil" claim is meaningless without this. Seek products with **≥500 mg combined EPA+DHA per capsule** (60%+ concentration). For freshness, look for products tested to the GOED standard: peroxide value ≤5, anisidine value ≤20, TOTOX ≤26. The **IFOS 5-star certification** is the gold standard for omega-3 purity testing, evaluating heavy metals, PCBs, dioxins, and oxidation markers batch by batch. Algal oil is a legitimate alternative for vegetarians, with DHA bioequivalent to fish-derived sources, though historically lower in EPA (newer strains are improving this).

The cardiovascular evidence remains genuinely complicated. REDUCE-IT showed a **25% reduction in major cardiovascular events** with high-dose pure EPA, but its mineral oil placebo may have inflated the benefit by worsening biomarkers in the control group. STRENGTH, using EPA+DHA with an inert corn oil placebo, found zero benefit at comparable EPA blood levels. Low-to-moderate dose supplementation (1–2 g/day) likely provides modest cardiovascular benefit, particularly for myocardial infarction risk. The strongest supplementation evidence exists for pregnant women — a Cochrane review found omega-3s reduced **early preterm birth by 42%** — and for people eating fewer than two servings of fatty fish per week. Individuals already consuming adequate fish likely see minimal additional benefit.

## Magnesium is the most underappreciated deficiency in Western diets

Half of American adults fail to meet the estimated average requirement for magnesium from food alone, per NHANES data. Globally, roughly 30% of the population has inadequate intake. Yet unlike vitamin D, where deficiency testing is debated, magnesium status is genuinely difficult to assess — **less than 1% of total body magnesium resides in blood**, making standard serum tests an unreliable marker. This subclinical inadequacy manifests subtly: impaired sleep, elevated blood pressure, increased insulin resistance, and compromised exercise recovery.

**The RDA is 400–420 mg/day for men and 310–320 mg/day for women**, with athletes likely needing 10–20% more due to sweat losses of 3–24 mg per liter and increased metabolic turnover. The tolerable upper limit from supplements is 350 mg/day of elemental magnesium — critically, this applies only to supplemental intake, not food. For blood pressure, meta-analyses show doses **≥400 mg/day for ≥12 weeks** reduce systolic pressure by 2–6 mmHg. For sleep, a 2025 RCT found magnesium bisglycinate improved sleep efficiency and reduced nighttime awakenings, while a 2024 RCT using magnesium L-threonate (1 g/day for 21 days) significantly improved insomnia severity scores. Depression meta-analyses show significant reductions at 248–500 mg elemental magnesium daily. However, the evidence for exercise performance enhancement in magnesium-replete individuals is weak, and a Cochrane review found **no benefit for muscle cramps** — one of the most common reasons people buy magnesium.

**Split your dose and separate from calcium.** Magnesium absorption is saturable — splitting 300 mg into two doses roughly doubles the amount actually absorbed compared to a single 600 mg bolus. Take it with food to reduce GI side effects, and separate from calcium supplements by at least two hours due to absorption competition. If sleep is the goal, take magnesium glycinate **30–60 minutes before bed**; glycine independently activates calming NMDA receptor pathways and may lower core body temperature. Magnesium is also a required cofactor for vitamin D metabolism, making co-supplementation with D particularly logical.

**The form you choose matters enormously — perhaps more than for any other supplement on this list.** The practical hierarchy is:

- **Magnesium glycinate/bisglycinate**: Best overall choice. High bioavailability via dipeptide transporter pathways, excellent GI tolerance, and glycine's calming properties make it ideal for general repletion and sleep. ~14% elemental magnesium.
- **Magnesium citrate**: Good bioavailability, well-studied, affordable. Mild laxative effect at higher doses. ~16% elemental magnesium.
- **Magnesium L-threonate (Magtein®)**: Unique blood-brain barrier penetration documented in clinical trials. Only ~7.5% elemental magnesium, so impractical as a sole magnesium source. Four published RCTs show cognitive and sleep benefits, but all were funded by the patent holder.
- **Magnesium oxide**: Despite containing ~60% elemental magnesium, absorption is as low as **4%** in some studies. It's the form most likely to cause diarrhea and the form used in the negative Cochrane cramp review. Not recommended for correcting deficiency.

A critical label-reading tip: some products marketed as "magnesium glycinate" actually contain magnesium oxide blended with free glycine — check the "Other Ingredients" section for hidden oxide. Look for "fully reacted" or "chelated" on the label. Third-party certification from USP, NSF Certified for Sport, or ConsumerLab adds assurance.

The populations most likely to benefit include the roughly half of adults with inadequate dietary intake, anyone on PPIs or diuretics (which deplete magnesium), older adults (decreased absorption plus medication interactions), athletes with high sweat rates, people with type 2 diabetes (25–38% are hypomagnesemic), and those with poor sleep who have not tried a well-absorbed form. People with consistently nutrient-dense diets rich in leafy greens, nuts, seeds, and legumes may genuinely not need supplementation. Those with impaired kidney function should avoid unsupervised magnesium supplementation entirely.

## Caffeine is the world's most effective legal performance enhancer

The ergogenic effects of caffeine are supported by evidence that is, frankly, overwhelming. A 2020 umbrella review of 21 meta-analyses in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* confirmed significant benefits for aerobic endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, power, jumping performance, and exercise speed. Endurance performance improves by **2–4% in time trials** — an enormous margin at competitive levels — while strength gains are smaller but still statistically meaningful (SMD = 0.18).

**The effective dose is 3–6 mg/kg body mass, taken 30–60 minutes before exercise.** For a 70 kg person, that's 210–420 mg. Emerging evidence suggests doses as low as **2 mg/kg** (~140 mg) produce significant effects on strength and endurance, with effect sizes comparable to higher doses. Beyond 9 mg/kg, side effects increase sharply with no additional performance benefit. For prolonged endurance events, re-dosing with 1.5–3 mg/kg every 60–90 minutes is effective. Caffeinated gum can be used 10–15 minutes before exercise due to faster buccal absorption.

**Sleep management is the most underappreciated aspect of caffeine use.** A 2023 meta-analysis quantified the damage: caffeine reduces total sleep time by **45 minutes**, increases sleep onset latency by 9 minutes, and decreases deep sleep by 11 minutes. The practical cutoffs are striking — a standard coffee (~107 mg) should be consumed at least **8.8 hours before bedtime**, and a typical pre-workout supplement (~218 mg) at least **13.2 hours before bedtime**. A 2025 double-blind RCT confirmed that 400 mg impaired sleep even when consumed 12 hours before bed, and participants couldn't perceive the disruption subjectively. Caffeine withdrawal before competition is not recommended — the performance-impairing headaches and fatigue outweigh any marginal resensitization benefit.

**Caffeine anhydrous in capsule form provides the most precise and well-studied dosing.** A 2025 network meta-analysis found low-dose capsules were the most effective delivery method for time-trial performance. Coffee produces similar ergogenic effects when dose-matched but varies enormously in caffeine content (50–200+ mg per cup). Pre-workout supplements are concerning: a label-accuracy study found actual caffeine content ranged from **59% to 176% of claims**, and 44% of ingredients in top-selling products hide in proprietary blends. If using multi-ingredient pre-workouts, choose only those with transparent labels and third-party certification from NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport, or BSCG.

Genetics meaningfully modulate caffeine response. The CYP1A2 gene determines metabolism speed: **AA genotype** individuals (~46% of the population) are fast metabolizers who see the largest performance gains, while **CC genotype** (~10%) may actually *worsen* performance with caffeine. A cycling study found AA carriers improved by ~6.8% while CC carriers slowed by ~13.7%. Habitual users still benefit from acute caffeine supplementation — a 2022 meta-analysis of 60 studies confirmed this — but the magnitude is reduced, with naive-to-mild consumers showing roughly four times larger effects on movement velocity than heavy users. Both trained and untrained individuals respond, and the evidence is remarkably consistent across sports, though endurance disciplines see the largest effects.

## Conclusion: what the evidence actually demands you do differently

The research converges on a few principles that cut across all six supplements. **Consistency trumps timing for every single one** — daily creatine intake matters more than peri-workout timing, total daily protein matters more than the anabolic window, and chronic omega-3 membrane incorporation matters more than taking fish oil before training. **Form selection is decisive for magnesium and omega-3s** — magnesium oxide and low-concentration fish oil capsules are among the most common purchases and among the least effective, meaning millions of consumers are supplementing without benefit due to poor product choice rather than poor science. **Deficiency correction drives the largest effect sizes** across vitamin D, magnesium, and omega-3s; once levels are adequate, additional supplementation shows rapidly diminishing returns.

Three supplements earn near-universal recommendation for active individuals: creatine monohydrate (3–5 g/day), adequate total protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg/day from any combination of food and powder), and strategic caffeine (3–6 mg/kg pre-exercise with appropriate sleep cutoffs). The other three — vitamin D, omega-3s, and magnesium — are best guided by individual risk factors: latitude and skin tone for D, fish intake for omega-3s, and dietary quality plus medication use for magnesium. The most impactful change most readers can make isn't adding a new supplement — it's switching to properly absorbed forms of what they already take and verifying that their products carry third-party certification.
