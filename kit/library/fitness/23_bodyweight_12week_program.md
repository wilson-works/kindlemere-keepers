# The complete 12-week bodyweight program for the formerly inactive

**This program takes you from deconditioned beginner to capable intermediate in 12 weeks using zero equipment — just your body, a floor, a wall, and a sturdy table or door frame.** The design follows ACSM and NSCA guidelines for novice resistance training: starting with conservative volume (2 sets per exercise, 3 days per week) and building to intermediate-level frequency (5 days, 3–4 sets) by Week 12. Every phase accounts for the stiffness and limited range of motion that come with prolonged inactivity, layering in targeted mobility work so your body can actually perform each movement correctly before you're asked to do it harder. The program prioritizes general fitness — a blend of strength, muscular endurance, and movement quality — rather than chasing any single adaptation.

Research from Schoenfeld et al. (2021) confirms that bodyweight training taken close to failure stimulates meaningful strength and hypertrophy gains across a wide rep range (**6–30 reps**), and Kotarsky et al. (2018) demonstrated that progressive calisthenics variations produce upper-body strength gains comparable to bench pressing. The progression framework here uses "mechanical advantage manipulation" — shifting your body position to increase or decrease difficulty — so you always train within the **8–15 rep sweet spot** that overlaps strength, hypertrophy, and endurance adaptations.

## 1. Program overview: your weekly schedule across three phases

Each four-week phase increases training frequency, volume, and exercise difficulty. The split rotates through four movement categories — Push (chest, shoulders, triceps), Pull (back, biceps, rear delts), Legs (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves), and Core (anterior core, obliques, lower back) — combined strategically to fit each phase's available days.

### Phase 1 — Weeks 1–4: building the foundation (3 days per week)

| Day | Focus | Rationale |
|-----|-------|-----------|
| **Monday** | Push + Core | Pair upper-body pressing with core stability |
| Tuesday | Rest | |
| **Wednesday** | Legs + Pull | Pair lower body with back/pulling work |
| Thursday | Rest | |
| **Friday** | Full Body (all four categories, lighter) | Reinforce movement patterns, add weekly volume |
| Saturday–Sunday | Rest | Full recovery for deconditioned tissue |

**Volume:** 2 sets per exercise, **8–12 rep target**. Rest **90 seconds** between sets. Total session time including warm-up: 35–45 minutes.

### Phase 2 — Weeks 5–8: expanding capacity (4 days + 1 active recovery)

| Day | Focus | Rationale |
|-----|-------|-----------|
| **Monday** | Push | Dedicated pressing volume |
| **Tuesday** | Legs | Full lower-body session |
| Wednesday | Rest | |
| **Thursday** | Pull + Core | Back work paired with core training |
| **Friday** | Full Body | Higher-intensity compound session |
| **Saturday** | Active Recovery | Light movement, mobility, foam rolling |
| Sunday | Rest | |

**Volume:** 3 sets per exercise, **8–15 rep target**. Rest **60–90 seconds** (accessories) to **90–120 seconds** (compounds). Session time: 40–50 minutes.

### Phase 3 — Weeks 9–12: intermediate intensity (5 days + 1 active recovery + 1 rest)

| Day | Focus | Rationale |
|-----|-------|-----------|
| **Monday** | Push | Heavy pressing emphasis |
| **Tuesday** | Pull | Dedicated back/pulling volume |
| **Wednesday** | Legs | Full lower body |
| **Thursday** | Active Recovery | Mobility, walking, foam rolling |
| **Friday** | Core + Push (secondary) | Core emphasis with supplemental pressing |
| **Saturday** | Pull + Legs (secondary) | Back emphasis with supplemental lower body |
| Sunday | Full Rest | Complete recovery |

**Volume:** 3–4 sets per exercise, **6–15 rep target** (varying by exercise difficulty). Rest **60–120 seconds**. Session time: 45–55 minutes.

## 2. The exercise library: every movement, regression, and progression

Each exercise below is assigned a **level number** within its category. You start at the level matching your current ability and advance when you hit the progression milestone. The standard rule, drawn from the Reddit Bodyweight Fitness Recommended Routine and corroborated by NSCA progression principles: **when you can complete 3 sets of 12 reps with controlled form and full range of motion on two consecutive sessions, advance to the next level.** For isometric holds, advance when you can hold **3 × 30 seconds**.

### Push exercises (chest, shoulders, triceps)

**Horizontal pressing chain:**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Wall Push-Ups** | Stand 3–4 feet from wall, hands at chest height, lower forehead to wall. Keep core braced. | If shoulders are tight, place hands slightly below shoulder height. Use partial range of motion and increase depth weekly. |
| 2 | **Incline Push-Ups (high surface)** | Hands on kitchen counter or sturdy table. Straight body line from head to heels. | Tight chest: perform doorway stretch for 30 sec/side beforehand. Wrist pain: make fists instead of flat hands. |
| 3 | **Incline Push-Ups (low surface)** | Hands on a chair seat or couch arm. Elbows at roughly 45° from torso. | Same regressions as above; reduce depth if shoulder pinches. |
| 4 | **Kneeling Push-Ups** | Knees on floor, hips extended (no "sagging"). Touch chest to floor. | Place a pillow under knees. Start with half range of motion if full depth causes shoulder discomfort. |
| 5 | **Standard Push-Ups** | Hands under shoulders, body rigid, lower until chest touches floor. Elbows 45° from body. | If you can do 3×6 kneeling but not 3×1 standard, do **negatives**: lower yourself over 5 seconds, drop to knees, push up from knees. |
| 6 | **Diamond Push-Ups** | Hands together forming a diamond under the chest. Elbows track close to body. Emphasizes triceps. | Elevate hands on a low step to reduce load if too difficult. |
| 7 | **Decline Push-Ups** | Feet elevated on chair or bed. Increases upper-chest and shoulder demand. | Use a low surface (6–8 inches) initially and increase height over weeks. |
| 8 | **Archer Push-Ups** | Wide grip, shift weight to one arm; the other arm extends straight as you lower. Train both sides. | Use an incline surface until you can perform 3×8 flat. |

**Vertical pressing chain (shoulders):**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Incline Pike Push-Ups** | Hands on a chair, feet on floor, hips high in an inverted-V. Lower forehead toward the floor between hands. | Limited overhead mobility: skip this category until you can raise arms fully overhead without low-back arching. Perform wall slides daily. |
| 2 | **Pike Push-Ups** | Same shape on the floor. Walk feet closer to hands for steeper angle. Head touches floor between hands. | Start with a wide foot stance for balance. Reduce depth if shoulders pinch overhead. |
| 3 | **Decline Pike Push-Ups** | Feet elevated on chair in pike position. Steep angle approaches handstand mechanics. | Only attempt after 3×12 flat pike push-ups AND passing the wall overhead reach test (arms fully overhead, back flat against wall). |

### Pull exercises (no equipment required)

This is the most challenging category without a pull-up bar. These progressions use the floor, a sturdy door frame, and a table to build back strength effectively.

**Floor pulling chain (no equipment at all):**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Prone Y-Raises** | Lie face down, arms overhead in a Y-shape, thumbs up. Lift arms and chest 2–3 inches. Squeeze shoulder blades. Hold 2 sec at top. | Rounded shoulders: start with tiny range of motion — even 1 inch counts. Focus on scapular retraction, not height. |
| 2 | **Prone I-Y-T-W Combo** | Cycle through arms straight overhead (I), Y-shape, T-shape (arms to sides), W-shape (elbows bent). Hold each position 3 sec. | Tight lats: perform child's pose lat stretch for 30 sec between sets. |
| 3 | **Superman Pulls** | From superman position (arms extended, chest lifted), pull elbows down to your sides mimicking a lat pulldown. Squeeze for 2 sec. Extend back out. | Start with chest resting on floor; only lift arms through the pull. |
| 4 | **Reverse Snow Angels** | Lie prone, arms at sides. Sweep arms overhead in an arc while keeping them off the ground. Reverse back to sides. Slow and controlled. | If shoulders fatigue quickly, do half-range (arms to T-position only) and build to full overhead sweep. |

**Row progression (using door frame or sturdy table):**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Doorway Rows** | Grip both sides of an open door frame, feet close to the base, lean back, and pull chest toward the frame. The more upright your body angle, the easier. | Stand nearly vertical to start. Focus on squeezing shoulder blades together rather than pulling hard. |
| 2 | **Towel Rows** | Loop a towel around a sturdy post or closed door handle. Lean back and row. Pinch shoulder blades back and down at peak contraction. | Keep a high body angle (nearly standing). Add a 2-sec hold at the top for scapular activation. |
| 3 | **Table Rows (bent-knee)** | Lie under a sturdy table, grip the edge, pull chest to the table with knees bent and feet flat. Keep body straight. **Confirm table stability before every set.** | Start with a very high table (like a desk) and your body at a steep angle. |
| 4 | **Table Rows (straight-leg)** | Same setup, but extend legs fully. Dramatically harder because more bodyweight is in play. | If too difficult, keep one knee bent (one leg straight, one bent) as a half-step. |
| 5 | **Elevated-Feet Table Rows** | Place feet on a chair while rowing under the table. Maximum horizontal-row difficulty without a bar. | Only progress here after 3×12 straight-leg table rows. |

**Isometric back work (supplementary, used in all phases):**
- **Wall Back Press**: Stand with back to wall, press elbows and back of hands into wall as hard as possible. Hold 15–30 sec. Builds scapular retractor endurance with zero mobility requirement.
- **Self-Resisted Towel Pulls**: Hold a towel taut between both hands. Perform a rowing motion while resisting with the opposite hand. Creates self-generated resistance.

### Leg exercises (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves)

**Squat (quad-dominant) progression:**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Assisted Squats** | Hold a door frame or chair back for balance. Squat to the depth you can manage with heels down. | Limited ankle mobility: place heels on a book (1–2 inches). Tight hip flexors: perform kneeling hip flexor stretch for 30 sec/side first. |
| 2 | **Box Squats** | Squat down to sit on a chair or couch, pause 1 sec, stand back up. Teaches depth control. | Use a higher surface initially, then progressively lower (stack of pillows removed one at a time). |
| 3 | **Bodyweight Squats** | Full depth (thighs at least parallel), heels down, arms forward for counterbalance. | If heels rise, continue using heel elevation until ankle mobility improves (typically 3–4 weeks of daily ankle rockers). |
| 4 | **Split Squats** | Staggered stance, lower back knee toward floor. Front knee stays over ankle. | Use a wall or chair for balance. Shorten stride length to reduce hip-flexor stretch demand. |
| 5 | **Bulgarian Split Squats** | Rear foot elevated on chair or couch, lower until back knee nearly touches floor. | Hold a door frame for balance. Start with a low surface (6-inch step) and increase elevation. |
| 6 | **Step-Ups** | Step onto a sturdy chair with one leg, drive through heel, control the descent. | Use a lower step (staircase step). Add a pause at the top. |
| 7 | **Assisted Pistol Squats** | Single-leg squat to a chair (sit and stand on one leg). Reduce chair height over weeks. | Hold door frame or wall with one hand. |
| 8 | **Pistol Squats** | Full single-leg squat with opposite leg extended forward. Requires strength, mobility, and balance. | Continue using box/chair assist until you can do 3×5 unassisted. |

**Hinge (hamstring/glute-dominant) progression:**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Glute Bridges** | Lie on back, feet flat, drive hips to full extension. Squeeze glutes hard at top for 2 sec. | Tight hip flexors: this exercise is your best friend — it strengthens glutes while reciprocally inhibiting tight hip flexors. Keep a posterior pelvic tilt throughout. |
| 2 | **Glute Bridge March** | Hold bridge position, alternate lifting one foot 2 inches off the floor. Don't let hips drop or rotate. | If hips rotate, hold the standard bridge longer (3×30 sec) before progressing. |
| 3 | **Single-Leg Glute Bridge** | One leg extended toward ceiling, drive up with the other leg. Don't let hips sag on the extended side. | Bend the non-working leg and rest it across the working thigh (figure-4 position) to reduce difficulty. |
| 4 | **Reverse Lunges** | Step backward, lower back knee toward floor, push through front heel to return. | Hold wall or chair. Shorten the step-back distance if hip flexors complain. |
| 5 | **Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift** | Stand on one leg, hinge forward with flat back, rear leg extends behind. Touch fingertips to shin-level. | Hold a wall with one hand for balance. Limit hinge depth to what your hamstrings allow without rounding the back. |
| 6 | **Sliding Leg Curls** | Lie on back, heels on a towel on smooth floor (or in socks). Bridge up, slide heels away, curl back in. | Start with very short slide distance (6 inches). Progress range over sessions. |
| 7 | **Nordic Curl Negatives** | Kneel with feet anchored under a heavy couch. Slowly lower body forward over **5+ seconds**. Catch yourself with hands and push back to start. | The negative-only version is the regression. Take 8–10 seconds per rep initially. If you can't control even a slow negative, continue with sliding leg curls. |

**Calves:** Perform double-leg calf raises (3×15–20) on a step edge in Phase 1–2, progressing to single-leg calf raises (3×10–15) in Phase 3. Lower heels below the step for full range of motion. Hold a wall for balance.

### Core exercises (anterior core, obliques, lower back)

**Anti-extension progression (front core):**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Dead Bugs (bent knee)** | Lie on back, arms vertical, knees at 90°. Lower opposite arm and leg simultaneously. **Press lower back into the floor throughout** — this is the most important cue. Exhale as limbs extend. | Tight lower back: this is the safest starting exercise. If you can't maintain back contact, reduce the range — only lower limbs a few inches. |
| 2 | **Dead Bugs (straight leg)** | Same pattern but extend the leg fully. Longer lever = harder core demand. | Keep the non-moving leg bent at 90°. Only straighten as far as your back stays flat on the floor. |
| 3 | **Kneeling Plank** | Forearms and knees. Straight line from knees to head. Hold for time. | Place a pillow under knees. Start with 15-sec holds if 30 sec is too much. |
| 4 | **Full Plank** | Forearms and toes. Squeeze glutes, brace abs as if bracing for a punch. Don't let hips sag or tent. | Build to 3×30 sec of kneeling plank before attempting. Start full plank with 10–15 sec holds. |
| 5 | **Tuck Hollow Hold** | Lie on back, knees tucked to chest, shoulders off ground, lower back pressed to floor. Arms at sides. | If neck strains, keep head down. Focus purely on pressing the lower back into the floor. |
| 6 | **Hollow Body Hold** | Arms overhead, legs extended 4–6 inches off floor, lower back cemented to the ground. Banana shape. **20–40 sec** holds. | Extend one leg at a time. Keep arms at your sides (not overhead) until you can hold 3×20 sec. |
| 7 | **Hollow Body Rocks** | Maintain hollow position, gently rock back and forth. Hands and feet never touch ground. | Master static hold for 3×30 sec before adding the rocking motion. |

**Anti-rotation and oblique progression:**

| Level | Exercise | Beginner cue | Regression for stiff individuals |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Bird Dogs** | On all fours, extend opposite arm and leg. Hold 3 sec. Keep hips perfectly level — place a water bottle on your lower back as a feedback tool. | If balance is poor, extend only the arm OR only the leg, not both simultaneously. |
| 2 | **Kneeling Side Plank** | Forearm and bottom knee. Stack hips. Hold 20–30 sec each side. | Place top hand on the floor in front for support initially. |
| 3 | **Side Plank** | Forearm and stacked feet. Body in a straight line. 30–45 sec each side. | Stagger feet (top foot in front) for a wider base. Build from kneeling version first. |
| 4 | **Side Plank with Hip Dips** | From side plank, lower hip toward floor and raise back up. 8–12 reps per side. | Perform from the kneeling side plank position. |

**Flexion progression (supplementary):**

| Level | Exercise | Notes |
|-------|----------|-------|
| 1 | **Lying Knee Raises** | Bring knees to chest, lower with control. Keep lower back down. |
| 2 | **Reverse Crunches** | Curl hips off the floor at the top of each knee raise. |
| 3 | **V-Ups (tuck)** | Simultaneously lift torso and tuck knees to meet in the middle. |
| 4 | **V-Ups (straight leg)** | Full extension. Hands reach toward toes at top. |

## 3. Week-by-week workout plans with sets, reps, and rest

Below is the complete programming for all 12 weeks. **Exercise levels** reference the library above. Choose the level that lets you work within the prescribed rep range. If the level listed is too hard, drop one level; too easy, advance one level.

### Phase 1 — Weeks 1–4

**Effort guideline:** Stay **2–4 reps from failure** (RPE 6–8). The goal is learning movement patterns and building work capacity, not grinding to exhaustion. The repeated bout effect means even conservative volume drives adaptation in untrained muscle.

#### Week 1 — Introduction week (reduced volume)

All exercises: **2 sets**, resting **90 seconds** between sets. This intentionally low volume triggers the repeated bout effect while minimizing DOMS.

**Monday — Push + Core:**

| Exercise | Level | Sets × Reps | Rest |
|----------|-------|-------------|------|
| Wall Push-Ups or Incline Push-Ups (high) | 1–2 | 2 × 8–10 | 90 sec |
| Incline Pike Push-Ups | 1 | 2 × 6–8 | 90 sec |
| Dead Bugs (bent knee) | 1 | 2 × 8/side | 60 sec |
| Kneeling Plank | 3 | 2 × 15–20 sec hold | 60 sec |
| Bird Dogs | 1 | 2 × 6/side (3-sec hold) | 60 sec |

**Wednesday — Legs + Pull:**

| Exercise | Level | Sets × Reps | Rest |
|----------|-------|-------------|------|
| Assisted Squats or Box Squats | 1–2 | 2 × 8–10 | 90 sec |
| Glute Bridges | 1 | 2 × 10–12 | 90 sec |
| Prone I-Y-T-W Combo | 2 | 2 × 5 cycles (3-sec holds) | 60 sec |
| Doorway Rows | 1 | 2 × 8–10 | 90 sec |
| Double-Leg Calf Raises | — | 2 × 12–15 | 60 sec |

**Friday — Full Body (lighter):**

| Exercise | Level | Sets × Reps | Rest |
|----------|-------|-------------|------|
| Wall Push-Ups or Incline Push-Ups | 1–2 | 2 × 8–10 | 90 sec |
| Doorway Rows | 1 | 2 × 8–10 | 90 sec |
| Assisted/Box Squats | 1–2 | 2 × 8–10 | 90 sec |
| Glute Bridges | 1 | 2 × 10–12 | 60 sec |
| Dead Bugs (bent knee) | 1 | 2 × 6/side | 60 sec |

#### Weeks 2–4 — Progressive build

Keep the same exercises but progress volume and reps each week. Switch exercises only when you hit the **3×12 milestone**.

| Variable | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
|----------|--------|--------|--------|
| **Sets per exercise** | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| **Rep target** | 8–10 | 10–12 | 10–12 (increase level if hitting 12 easily) |
| **Rest between sets** | 90 sec | 90 sec | 60–90 sec |
| **Isometric holds** | 20 sec | 25 sec | 30 sec |
| **Effort level** | RPE 6–7 | RPE 7 | RPE 7–8 |

**Specific exercise progression through Weeks 2–4:** By Week 3, most people should advance at least one level in push (e.g., from wall push-ups to incline push-ups) and squats (from assisted to box or full bodyweight squats). If you hit **3×12** in Week 2, progress the exercise level in Week 3.

The **session structure remains identical** to Week 1 — same days, same exercise slots — but with updated sets/reps and potentially harder exercise variations based on your progress.

**Key progressions during Weeks 6–8:** Most trainees should reach standard push-ups (Level 5), table rows with straight legs (Level 4), bodyweight squats to full depth (Level 3), and full planks for 3×30 sec (Level 4). If you are progressing faster, begin incorporating diamond push-ups, Bulgarian split squats, and hollow body holds.

#### Weeks 10–12 — Final push

| Variable | Week 10 | Week 11 | Week 12 |
|----------|---------|---------|---------|
| **Sets per exercise** | 3–4 | 4 | 4 (deload to 2–3 on Week 12 if fatigued) |
| **Rep target** | 8–12 | 8–15 | 8–15 |
| **Rest between sets** | 60–120 sec | 60–120 sec | 60–120 sec |
| **Isometric holds** | 30–40 sec | 35–45 sec | 30–45 sec |
| **Effort level** | RPE 8–9 | RPE 9 | RPE 8 (slight deload) |
| **Tempo** | 3-sec eccentric on compounds | 3-sec eccentric | Normal (deload) |

**Week 12 functions as an auto-regulated deload**: if accumulated fatigue is high (performance dropping, persistent soreness, poor sleep), reduce sets to 2–3 per exercise and stop every set 3–4 reps from failure. Research from Bell et al. (2023) confirms that **reducing volume by 40–50% while maintaining frequency** preserves strength and hypertrophy while clearing fatigue. If you feel strong, train through at full intensity — beginners accumulate less systemic fatigue than advanced athletes and may not need the deload.

## 4. Mobility and warm-up routine to dismantle stiffness from inactivity

Sedentary living creates a predictable pattern: **tight hip flexors, locked thoracic spine, shortened chest muscles, stiff ankles, and weak glutes.** A 2024 University of South Australia meta-analysis of 188 studies found that flexibility gains max out at **3 minutes of stretching per muscle per session** and **8 minutes per muscle per week** — so the routines below are designed to hit that dose efficiently.

### Pre-workout dynamic warm-up (8–12 minutes, every session)

Perform this sequence before every training session. It raises core temperature, lubricates joints, and primes the movement patterns you're about to train.

**General warm-up (3 minutes):** March in place for 30 seconds, then transition to high knees for 30 seconds, butt kicks for 30 seconds, jumping jacks for 30 seconds, and arm circles (forward and backward) for 30 seconds each direction. The goal is to break a light sweat.

**Dynamic mobility circuit (5–7 minutes):** Move through these continuously, spending roughly 30–40 seconds per drill.

| Drill | Target area | Reps/Duration |
|-------|-------------|---------------|
| Cat-Cow | Thoracic spine, lumbar spine | 8–10 slow reps |
| Quadruped Thoracic Rotations | Thoracic spine, shoulders | 6 each side |
| Hip CARs (standing, hold wall) | Hip joint full ROM | 3–5 each direction/side |
| Kneeling Hip Flexor Stretch with Glute Squeeze | Hip flexors, glutes | 20 sec each side |
| Leg Swings (front-to-back) | Hamstrings, hip flexors | 10 each leg |
| Leg Swings (side-to-side) | Adductors, abductors | 10 each leg |

**Day-specific additions (1–2 minutes):**

## 5. Progression criteria for moving between phases

Advancement between phases is not purely time-based. You should meet specific readiness benchmarks before progressing. If you don't meet all criteria, **repeat the final week** of the current phase until you do.

### From Phase 1 to Phase 2 (after Week 4)

You should be able to perform all of the following before moving to four training days:

| Benchmark | Standard | Why it matters |
|-----------|----------|----------------|
| Kneeling push-ups | 3 × 12 with full ROM | Confirms sufficient pressing strength for standard push-up progression |
| Bodyweight squats | 3 × 12 to at least parallel depth | Demonstrates adequate hip/ankle mobility and leg strength |
| Glute bridges | 3 × 15 with 2-sec hold at top | Baseline glute activation and hip extension |
| Doorway/towel rows | 3 × 12 | Back strength foundation for harder row progressions |
| Full plank | 1 × 30 seconds | Baseline core stability |
| Dead bugs | 3 × 10/side with back flat | Core control and coordination |
| Session recovery | No soreness lasting beyond 48 hours after training | Indicates adequate recovery capacity for increased frequency |
| Consistency | Completed at least 10 of 12 scheduled sessions | Habit formation and readiness for more frequent training |

### From Phase 2 to Phase 3 (after Week 8)

| Benchmark | Standard | Why it matters |
|-----------|----------|----------------|
| Standard push-ups | 3 × 10 | Confirms full bodyweight pressing capacity |
| Table rows (bent-knee) | 3 × 12 | Horizontal pulling strength |
| Split squats | 3 × 10/side | Unilateral leg strength and balance |
| Single-leg glute bridges | 3 × 10/side | Posterior chain strength and stability |
| Full plank | 3 × 30 seconds | Robust core endurance |
| Side plank | 2 × 20 seconds each side | Lateral stability |
| Pike push-ups | 3 × 6 | Entry-level overhead pressing capacity |
| Ankle dorsiflexion | Can squat to full depth without heel elevation | Mobility improvement sufficient for advanced leg work |
| Recovery capacity | Can train 4 days/week without persistent fatigue or declining performance | Ready for 5-day frequency |

### Within-phase exercise progression rules

These rules apply at any point during the program:

**For dynamic exercises:** When you complete **3 sets of 12 reps** with controlled tempo (no bouncing, no partial reps) on two consecutive sessions → advance to the next exercise level. You will likely drop to 3×6–8 at the new level. Build back to 3×12 and repeat.

**For isometric holds:** When you hold **3 sets of 30 seconds** with correct form (no shaking, no compensatory movement) → advance to the next exercise level.

**For tempo work:** If an exercise is too easy but the next level is too hard (you can't get 3×5), add a **3–5 second eccentric** (lowering phase) to the current exercise. This typically adds 2–4 weeks of productive training at the same variation.

**When to stay put:** If you have been stuck at the same level for 3+ weeks, check these factors before forcing a progression: sleep quality (need 7–9 hours), nutrition (adequate protein — roughly **0.7–1.0 g per pound bodyweight** daily), and stress levels. Often a plateau breaks by improving recovery rather than training harder.

## 6. Active recovery day programming

Active recovery days serve two purposes: **maintain the exercise habit** and **promote blood flow to speed tissue repair** without adding training stress. Research shows active recovery provides significant subjective benefits (reduced stiffness, improved perceived recovery) even though its physiological superiority over passive rest remains debated.

### Your active recovery template (30–40 minutes total)

**Block 1 — Light movement (15–20 minutes):** Choose one activity and maintain a **conversational pace** (you can talk in full sentences without gasping). Heart rate should stay at **50–60% of maximum** (roughly 100–120 BPM for most beginners).

Options include a walk outdoors (the best option for mental health and vitamin D), an easy bike ride, gentle swimming, or a slow-paced household activity like gardening. The key constraint is that you should finish feeling better than when you started — not tired.

**Block 2 — Foam rolling or self-massage (8–10 minutes):** Using a foam roller, tennis ball, or even a water bottle, roll slowly over each area for 60–90 seconds. Target pressure is a **7 out of 10** on the discomfort scale — firm but tolerable. When you hit a tender spot, pause and breathe into it for 30 seconds.

Roll these areas in order: calves, quads, hamstrings, glutes (use a tennis ball), upper back/thoracic spine (lie on the roller and extend over it), and lats (lie on your side with the roller under your armpit).

**Block 3 — Dedicated mobility work (10–15 minutes):** Perform the dedicated mobility session from Section 4. Prioritize whatever feels tightest. On active recovery days, you can hold stretches longer (45–60 seconds) since there is no subsequent training to worry about.

**Block 4 — Breathing and cool-down (2–3 minutes):** Lie on your back, legs elevated on a couch or wall (legs-up-the-wall position). Perform **box breathing**: inhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds, exhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds. Repeat 5–8 cycles. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system and primes recovery.

### What to avoid on active recovery days

Do not turn the active recovery day into an extra training session. Avoid any exercise that causes significant muscular fatigue or elevates your heart rate above 60% of maximum for extended periods. Skip plyometrics, sprinting, or "just a few sets" of push-ups. The discipline of true easy days is what enables hard training days to be productive.

## Conclusion: the principles behind the program

Three ideas drive every decision in this program. First, **mechanical advantage progression** replaces the barbell's load increments — by shifting your body angle or moving from two limbs to one, you create the progressive overload that forces adaptation without ever touching a weight. Second, **mobility and strength develop simultaneously**, not sequentially — you don't need to "fix" your stiffness before training; every warm-up, every full-range rep, and every recovery session chips away at restriction while building capacity. Third, **consistency beats intensity** — the conservative Week 1 volume, the auto-regulated deload in Week 12, and the structured recovery days all exist to keep you training without interruption, because the research is unambiguous: the greatest predictor of results is showing up.

By Week 12, you should be performing standard push-ups, table rows, Bulgarian split squats, hollow body holds, and pike push-ups — movements that were likely impossible on Day 1. More importantly, you will have built the movement vocabulary, joint resilience, and recovery habits that make the next 12 weeks — and the 12 after that — even more productive.
